# Paper 4 — Case-Law Digest for BNS/K-1006

**Purpose:** Exam-ready case briefs, with a clear distinction between (a) binding legal principles developed under the former IPC or constitutional law and (b) the current BNS statutory wording that must control the answer.

**Verification note:** The propositions below are study summaries. Before citing in a final answer, confirm the reported citation, exact holding, later treatment and relevance to the precise BNS provision from the judgment itself. Do not present an IPC case as if it construed the BNS.

## 1. General criminal liability and mens rea

### *Nathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1966) — mens rea
- **Facts in brief:** The accused was prosecuted in a regulatory/licensing context involving the storage of food grains and an alleged licensing breach.
- **Issue:** Whether criminal liability arose without the requisite guilty mind on the facts and statutory scheme.
- **Principle:** Mens rea is ordinarily presumed to be an element of a criminal offence unless excluded expressly or by necessary implication; statutory purpose and wording matter.
- **Exam use:** Explain the general role of mens rea and then ask whether the particular BNS offence specifies intention, knowledge, rashness, negligence or another mental state. Do not use the case to override clear statutory language.

## 2. Attempt

### *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961) — preparation vs attempt
- **Issue:** When acts have progressed beyond preparation toward commission of the intended offence.
- **Principle:** Attempt is assessed from the acts done, the intention and their relation to the completed offence; no single physical-proximity formula resolves every case.
- **Exam use:** Set out the intended offence, mental element, acts completed and remaining steps; apply BNS s. 62 and any offence-specific attempt provision.

### *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980) — proximity and attempt
- **Issue:** Whether conduct directed toward an offence was sufficiently proximate to constitute an attempt.
- **Principle:** The court evaluates the whole course of conduct and proximity to the intended offence; conduct need not be the final act in every case.
- **Exam use:** Contrast preparation and attempt with concrete facts. Identify any statutory offence that criminalises preparation separately.

## 3. Culpable homicide and murder

### *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya* (1976) — culpable homicide and murder
- **Issue:** The relationship between culpable homicide and murder under the former IPC.
- **Principle:** Murder is the aggravated form within the broader category of culpable homicide; the mental-state and circumstance tests, including exceptions, determine classification.
- **Exam use:** First prove death and causation; then apply BNS ss. 100–105 in sequence. Do not simply copy the former IPC section numbers or assume every old phrase maps word-for-word to BNS.

### *Virsa Singh v State of Punjab* (1958) — intentional injury and murder
- **Issue:** The ingredients of a murder clause concerning an intentionally inflicted bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death under the former IPC.
- **Principle:** The prosecution must prove the statutory elements relating to the injury and its sufficiency; the court assesses the injury and the accused's intention to inflict it.
- **Exam use:** Useful for injury-focused analysis, but match each ingredient to the actual BNS murder provision and current statutory wording.

## 4. Private defence

### *Darshan Singh v State of Punjab* (2010) — limits of private defence
- **Issue:** The scope and limits of private defence in a violent confrontation.
- **Principle:** Private defence is a protective, not punitive, right. It is assessed in the circumstances as they reasonably appeared; a person under attack is not expected to measure defensive force with mathematical precision, but the right is not a licence for revenge.
- **Exam use:** Apply BNS ss. 34–44 to imminence, necessity, nature of threat, proportionality in the statutory sense, commencement and cessation of the right.

## 5. Conspiracy and group liability

### *Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Administration)* (1988) — proof of conspiracy
- **Issue:** How criminal conspiracy may be proved where agreement is rarely established by direct evidence.
- **Principle:** Agreement may be inferred from coordinated conduct and surrounding circumstances, but the evidence must establish a meeting of minds; suspicion or association alone is insufficient.
- **Exam use:** Distinguish the agreement element of BNS s. 61 from mere presence, parallel conduct or friendship.

### *Pandurang, Tukia and Bhillia v State of Hyderabad* (1955) — common intention
- **Issue:** The requirements for joint liability based on common intention under the former IPC.
- **Principle:** Common intention is a distinct mental element inferred from the facts and conduct; it should not be assumed solely because several persons were present.
- **Exam use:** Apply BNS s. 3(5) and distinguish common intention from the common-object regime attached to unlawful assembly.

## 6. Cheating and contractual disputes

### *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar* (2000) — cheating vs breach of contract
- **Issue:** Whether failure to fulfil a promise establishes cheating.
- **Principle:** A later breach or non-performance does not by itself prove that dishonest intention existed when the promise was made. The initial deception and statutory inducement must be examined.
- **Exam use:** For BNS s. 318, identify the representation, falsity/deception, intention at the relevant time and the inducement or consequence specified by the provision.

## 7. Sentencing

### *Bachan Singh v State of Punjab* (1980) — death penalty
- **Issue:** The constitutional validity and permissible scope of capital punishment under the former penal framework.
- **Principle:** The death penalty was confined to the “rarest of rare” category under the doctrine developed by the Supreme Court, with sentencing requiring principled consideration of the case and offender.
- **Exam use:** For BNS sentencing questions, identify the punishment authorised by the specific provision and discuss proportionality and relevant mitigating/aggravating factors. Do not state that death is automatic for every offence carrying it.

## 8. Constitutional context for speech and public order

### *Kedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar* (1962) — historical sedition authority
- **Issue:** The constitutional limits on the former IPC sedition provision.
- **Principle:** The judgment interpreted the former provision in light of constitutional speech protections and public-order concerns.
- **Exam use:** Historical context only. Do **not** claim that the IPC sedition provision was simply renumbered. Analyse BNS s. 152's actual text and the constitutional issue independently.

## How to write a case in a 10- or 15-mark answer

Use **F-I-H-R-A-C**:
- **F — Facts:** two or three relevant sentences.
- **I — Issue:** the precise legal question.
- **H — Holding:** what the court decided.
- **R — Ratio:** the legal rule necessary to the decision.
- **A — Application:** why the principle matters to the question/problem.
- **C — Caution:** former IPC provision, dissent, later treatment or statutory change, if material.

A case name without a proposition and application adds little. One accurately explained case is generally more useful than a long list of names with no legal connection.
