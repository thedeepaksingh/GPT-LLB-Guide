## 1. Homicide: culpable homicide and murder

The central homicide analysis asks what the accused did, what harm resulted, what mental state existed, whether causation is established, and whether the facts fall within a murder provision or a statutory exception reducing liability.

A structured approach:
1. establish death and causation;
2. identify the act and the accused's role;
3. assess intention to cause death, intention to cause bodily injury of the relevant kind, or knowledge of likely consequences;
4. apply the statutory murder definition;
5. test each applicable exception and any partial defence;
6. state the resulting offence and sentencing range under BNS.

Do not reason that every intentional injury causing death is automatically murder. The statutory mental elements, injury, weapon, force, body part, surrounding circumstances and exceptions matter. Conversely, the absence of a desire to kill does not automatically exclude murder if the statutory test is otherwise met.

**Leading cases (principle, historically under IPC):**
- *Virsa Singh v State of Punjab* (1958): the bodily-injury limb of murder; the prosecution must prove the injury and the relevant intention as articulated in the judgment.
- *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya* (1976): explains the relationship between culpable homicide and murder.
- *K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra* (1962): provocation and the statutory exception in its historical context; apply the current BNS wording carefully.

## 2. Causing death by negligence

Negligence-based homicide differs from intentional homicide. Identify the degree and kind of negligence required by the applicable provision, causal link, and any special statutory rule. A tragic accident is not automatically a crime; nor does the label “accident” end the inquiry. The BNS includes a distinct provision on causing death by negligence; section 106(2) has been subject to a commencement exception, so check the latest notification and do not state it is in force without verification.

## 3. Hurt and grievous hurt

Distinguish simple hurt from grievous hurt by applying the statutory definitions, not colloquial severity. Relevant facts may include fracture/dislocation, permanent impairment, duration of severe pain or inability to follow ordinary pursuits, weapon used, intent/knowledge and whether the offence is aggravated. Medical evidence can be important, but the statutory definition determines classification.

For an answer: state the relevant definition, identify the injury, explain the mental element, check weapon/aggravating circumstances, then state the appropriate BNS provision and punishment.

## 4. Wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement

**Wrongful restraint** concerns voluntarily obstructing a person so as to prevent movement in a direction in which that person has a right to proceed, subject to statutory exceptions. **Wrongful confinement** is a more serious form involving restraint within circumscribed limits. The critical facts are the freedom of movement, the limits imposed, intention/voluntariness and any lawful authority.

## 5. Assault, criminal force and kidnapping/abduction

Do not equate assault with completed physical injury. The law may criminalise gestures or preparations creating apprehension of imminent criminal force. Criminal force concerns intentional use of force without consent for a prohibited purpose. Kidnapping and abduction have distinct statutory definitions and age/consent/jurisdictional elements; identify which definition applies before considering aggravated offences.

## 6. Sexual offences and consent

BNS contains a chapter on offences against women and children. For sexual offences, carefully identify the statutory act, consent definition, age, capacity, coercion, misconception, relationship, aggravating circumstances and applicable exceptions. Consent is a free and voluntary agreement to the specific act; absence of physical resistance alone is not proof of consent. Consent to one act is not consent to another, and a person unable to communicate consent cannot be presumed to have consented.

Sexual-offence answers must be legally precise and respectful. Distinguish:
- absence of consent;
- consent vitiated by a statutory circumstance;
- age-based rules;
- aggravated forms and enhanced punishment;
- evidentiary questions (BSA) from substantive offence definitions (BNS).

Do not conflate the criminal-law offence with the separate statutory framework under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO), where applicable. The exact age thresholds and statutory exceptions must be checked in the current Act and any special legislation.

## 7. Cruelty, dowry-related death and related offences

For offences involving a spouse, family cruelty or dowry-related death, identify the exact statutory ingredients: relationship, conduct, timing, nature of harassment, link to unlawful demand, causal circumstances and any presumptions under the evidence statute. A tragic death or marital dispute alone does not prove every element. Analyse each ingredient and distinguish substantive BNS liability from evidentiary presumptions under BSA.

## 8. Leading cases to understand (and limits)

- *State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh* (1996): sensitivity in evaluating testimony in sexual-offence cases; credibility must be assessed fairly and without stereotyped assumptions.
- *Lillu v State of Haryana* (2013): rejects the degrading “two-finger test” as a method inconsistent with dignity and bodily integrity.
- *Independent Thought v Union of India* (2017): constitutional treatment of child protection and marital rape exception for a wife below 18 under the then IPC framework; check current BNS text and binding subsequent law before stating the precise present rule.
- *Virsa Singh*, *Rayavarapu Punnayya* and *Nanavati*: homicide doctrines noted above.

## 9. Model answer plan: “Distinguish culpable homicide and murder”

Define both under the current BNS provisions; identify the mental-state hierarchy; explain that murder is the aggravated category subject to statutory exceptions; apply the facts to intention/knowledge, injury, causation and exception; use *Rayavarapu Punnayya* and *Virsa Singh* as interpretive authorities with a note that they interpreted IPC language; conclude with the likely offence, not a conclusory label.
