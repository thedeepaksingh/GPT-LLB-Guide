# Topic 3 — Abetment, Criminal Conspiracy, Attempt and Group Liability

**Paper:** CCSU LL.B. Semester I · K-1006 · BNS  
**Updated:** 2026-10-30 00:05 IST

## 1. Why liability can arise before the completed offence

Criminal law may punish participation in or movement toward an offence even where the substantive offence is not completed. The statutory basis differs between abetment, conspiracy, attempt and group-liability provisions. Do not treat these labels as interchangeable.

## 2. Abetment

BNS provisions beginning at section 45 address abetment. Broadly, abetment may occur by:
- instigating another person to do the act;
- engaging in a legally relevant conspiracy to do it, coupled with an act or illegal omission in pursuance where the statute requires it; or
- intentionally aiding the act by an act or illegal omission.

The exact definition and punishment depend on the provision and on whether the offence abetted is committed, is punishable with a specified penalty, or falls within a special rule. Mere presence, association or knowledge is not automatically intentional aid. The prosecution must prove the legally required participation and mental element.

**Instigation** involves intentional encouragement, provocation or urging of the prohibited act in the legal sense. Words must be assessed in context; a casual statement is not automatically abetment. **Intentional aid** requires more than accidental assistance.

## 3. Criminal conspiracy

BNS section 61 addresses criminal conspiracy. The central idea is an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. Depending on the object of the agreement, the statutory rule may require an overt act in pursuance or the agreement itself may complete the offence.

Conspiracy is usually proved by circumstances because agreements are rarely documented. But suspicion, friendship or parallel conduct alone is insufficient; the circumstances must support the inference of a meeting of minds. Each accused's connection to the agreement must be evaluated.

## 4. Attempt

BNS section 62 provides a general attempt framework, subject to offence-specific provisions. Attempt ordinarily requires the necessary intention/knowledge and an act that moves beyond preparation sufficiently toward commission. The offence need not succeed, but mere desire or planning is ordinarily insufficient.

A useful exam structure:
1. identify the completed offence intended;
2. establish the required mental state;
3. identify acts done;
4. assess whether the conduct crossed from preparation to attempt;
5. check whether a special attempt provision applies;
6. identify the punishment prescribed by the relevant section.

Authorities: *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961) and *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980).

## 5. Common intention / common object and group offences

Group liability may arise under the BNS's provisions on acts done by several persons with common intention, unlawful assembly and related offences. Exact statutory section numbers should be checked in the official Act before use. The analytical distinction:
- **Common intention:** a shared intention inferred from the participants' conduct and circumstances; participation and the statutory conditions must be established.
- **Common object:** attaches to members of an unlawful assembly where the statutory requirements are satisfied; it is not simply another name for common intention.
- **Individual act:** where no group-liability rule applies, the accused's own act and mental element must be proved.

The prosecution need not prove a written agreement or identical roles, but must establish the statutory elements for the form of group liability charged. Presence at the scene alone is not invariably sufficient.

## 6. Distinguish the doctrines

| Doctrine | Core idea | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Abetment | Instigation, conspiracy in the statutory sense, or intentional aid | Did the accused intentionally facilitate/encourage the offence in a recognised way? |
| Criminal conspiracy | Agreement to commit an illegal act or legal act by illegal means | Is there proof of a meeting of minds? |
| Attempt | Execution has progressed beyond preparation | Did the accused do an act sufficiently proximate to commission with the required mental state? |
| Common intention | Joint responsibility based on shared intention and statutory participation | Was there a shared intention and the required participation? |
| Common object | Liability connected to membership of an unlawful assembly and its object/knowledge | Were the statutory assembly conditions and object/knowledge established? |

## 7. Leading cases

- *Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Administration)* (1988): conspiracy can be inferred from circumstances, but agreement remains the essential element.
- *State of Maharashtra v Som Nath Thapa* (1996): explains the agreement and knowledge dimensions of conspiracy in its statutory context.
- *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961): preparation and attempt distinction.
- *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980): attempt is assessed by the acts and their proximity to the intended offence.
- *Pandurang, Tukia and Bhillia v State of Hyderabad* (1955): common intention requires a meeting of minds, though it may develop on the spot; apply the principle with the relevant current BNS provision.

These cases interpreted earlier penal-code provisions. Their reasoning may remain useful, but the current BNS text controls and any change in wording must be addressed.

## 8. Exam problem method

For a fact pattern involving several accused, make a separate mini-analysis for each person:
- conduct and role;
- required mental state;
- agreement, instigation, aid or participation evidence;
- stage of offence;
- possible defence or reasonable doubt;
- applicable BNS provision and punishment.

Avoid saying “all are guilty because they were together.” Explain the legal basis for attributing the act or agreement to each accused.

## 9. Model answer plan: “Distinguish abetment and conspiracy”

Define both; explain the statutory elements; identify the overlap where conspiracy is a mode of abetment but also a distinct offence; compare agreement versus instigation/intentional aid; explain proof; use *Kehar Singh*; conclude that agreement is central to conspiracy, while abetment can arise through other statutory modes.
