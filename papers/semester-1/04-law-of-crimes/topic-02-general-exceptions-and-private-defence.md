# Topic 2 — General Exceptions, Consent and Private Defence

**Paper:** CCSU LL.B. Semester I · K-1006 · BNS  
**Updated:** 2026-10-30 00:05 IST

## 1. Why general exceptions matter

An offence may appear complete at first glance, but a general exception can justify the conduct, excuse the actor, or negate a required mental element. BNS sections 14–44 contain important general exceptions and private-defence rules. Cite the precise subsection only after checking the current official Act text; the outline below is a study map.

## 2. Mistake of fact and authority of law

A mistake of fact may excuse conduct where the statutory conditions are satisfied; a mistake of law generally does not. Distinguish “I honestly believed the facts were different” from “I did not know the law prohibited it.” The belief must be assessed against the statutory requirement, including good faith where specified. A person cannot automatically rely on a fabricated or careless belief.

Judicial acts and acts done pursuant to court orders receive specific statutory treatment. The question is not simply whether the actor holds an official position; it is whether the act falls within the legal protection and its conditions.

## 3. Accident and necessity

**Accident:** The relevant provision protects an unintended consequence occurring while doing a lawful act, in a lawful manner, by lawful means and with proper care and caution, subject to statutory language. Lack of intent alone is not enough if the person acted negligently or unlawfully.

**Necessity / prevention of greater harm:** The law may recognise conduct undertaken without criminal intent to prevent a greater harm, but the statutory conditions and proportionality matter. Necessity is not a blanket permission to cause harm whenever it seems convenient.

## 4. Infancy

BNS provides a conclusive protection for a child below seven years and a qualified protection for a child above seven and below twelve who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding in the circumstances. Age and the child's capacity at the time are material. Do not import adult assumptions about intention without considering the specific rule.

## 5. Unsoundness of mind and intoxication

The unsoundness-of-mind exception concerns incapacity at the time of the act to know the nature of the act or that it is wrong/contrary to law, as stated in the statutory provision. A medical diagnosis alone does not automatically satisfy the legal test; the relevant capacity at the time must be established from the evidence.

Distinguish:
- **Involuntary intoxication:** may provide a defence where the statutory test is met.
- **Voluntary intoxication:** ordinarily does not operate as a general excuse; knowledge and intention may be assessed under the applicable statutory rules.
- **Specific intent offences:** analyse the exact mental element and statutory rule rather than assuming intoxication always helps or never matters.

## 6. Consent and acts done for benefit

Consent is not always a defence. Examine whether the person was legally capable of consenting, whether consent was free and informed where relevant, whether it was obtained by misconception or coercion, and whether the statute excludes consent as a defence. Consent cannot legalise every form of serious harm. Provisions concerning acts done for a person's benefit, medical treatment and good-faith conduct have their own limits.

## 7. Compulsion and trivial harm

A statutory exception may address conduct compelled by threats, but the exact provision and exclusions must be read carefully. It is not a defence to every serious offence. Likewise, the law may exclude harm so slight that a person of ordinary sense and temper would not complain; the threshold is objective and contextual, not simply the accused's view that the harm was small.

## 8. Private defence of body and property

Private defence is preventive, not punitive. Its rationale is that a person need not passively suffer unlawful aggression where immediate protection is reasonably necessary. The right is constrained by statutory limits:
- identify the imminent or legally relevant threat;
- distinguish defence of body from defence of property;
- assess whether the circumstances permit causing death or only lesser harm;
- use no more harm than the legal conditions permit;
- account for whether there was time to seek public protection and whether the threat had ended;
- do not confuse lawful defence with retaliation after the danger passes.

BNS sections 34–44 are the core private-defence range; verify the exact section for the precise rule and limits. The right generally does not justify inflicting unnecessary harm.

### Leading cases

- *Darshan Singh v State of Punjab* (2010): Supreme Court principles on private defence; the right is preventive and must be evaluated in the circumstances, without requiring a person under attack to calculate force with mathematical precision.
- *Munshi Ram v Delhi Administration* (1968): the right of private defence is a valuable right, but not a licence for aggression or revenge.
- *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat* (1964): burden and evidentiary treatment of the insanity defence under the former statutory framework; use for principle only after checking current law.

## 9. Method for analysing a defence problem

1. State the alleged offence and its ingredients.
2. Identify the particular exception and its statutory conditions.
3. Separate facts supporting the exception from mere assertion.
4. Evaluate timing, necessity, proportionality, good faith and capacity where relevant.
5. Explain the burden/standard applicable to the exception and distinguish it from the prosecution's burden.
6. Reach a reasoned conclusion; if facts are incomplete, state what further evidence is needed.

## 10. Model answer plan: “Discuss the right of private defence”

Define the right as preventive protection of body/property; identify the BNS statutory framework; explain commencement, imminence, necessity and limits; distinguish defence from retaliation; cite *Darshan Singh* and *Munshi Ram*; apply an illustration; conclude that the right is broad enough for effective defence but remains bounded by the statute.

## 11. Practice problems

**Problem A:** X sees Y raise a knife and advance. X pushes Y away, causing a fracture. Discuss whether X can invoke private defence.  
**Approach:** Determine imminence, reasonableness of the response, available alternatives, extent of force and evidence. A fracture does not alone settle lawfulness.

**Problem B:** A person causes a harmful result while performing an otherwise lawful act but has ignored an obvious safety risk.  
**Approach:** Test each statutory condition for accident, particularly proper care and caution; absence of intention is not conclusive.

**Problem C:** An accused claims a mental disorder but evidence suggests the person understood the act and its wrongfulness at the relevant time.  
**Approach:** Apply the legal capacity test at the time of conduct; distinguish diagnosis from statutory incapacity.
