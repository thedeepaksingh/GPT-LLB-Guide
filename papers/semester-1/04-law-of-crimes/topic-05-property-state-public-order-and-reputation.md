# Topic 5 — Property, State, Public Order, Documents and Reputation

**Paper:** CCSU LL.B. Semester I · K-1006 · BNS  
**Updated:** 2026-10-30 00:05 IST

## 1. Property offences: classify the conduct correctly

Property offences protect possession, ownership, security and lawful commercial dealings. The offence depends on the act and mental state—not simply on whether property changed hands.

### Theft
Identify movable property, possession, dishonest intention, movement and lack of consent as required by the BNS definition. Dishonesty generally concerns wrongful gain or wrongful loss. A genuine claim of right may be relevant to dishonest intention, but is not automatically decisive.

### Extortion
The core pattern is intentionally putting a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly inducing delivery of property, valuable security or another legally specified thing. Unlike ordinary theft, the victim's delivery is induced by fear.

### Robbery and dacoity
Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion meeting the statutory conditions involving violence, attempted violence or fear of instant harm. Dacoity depends on the statutory number of persons conjointly committing or attempting robbery, including the specified connected participants. Verify the exact threshold and section wording in the official BNS text.

### Criminal misappropriation and breach of trust
Misappropriation generally concerns dishonest conversion/use of property. Criminal breach of trust requires entrustment or dominion plus dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to the legal direction or contract. The entrustment element distinguishes breach of trust from many other property offences.

### Cheating
Cheating generally requires deception and dishonest/fraudulent inducement leading to delivery/retention of property or an act/omission causing or likely to cause the statutorily described harm. A later breach of contract does not, by itself, establish that dishonest intention existed at the beginning. Examine contemporaneous representations and intention.

### Mischief and criminal trespass
Mischief involves the prescribed intention/knowledge and wrongful destruction/change that diminishes value or utility or causes wrongful loss/damage. Criminal trespass centres on entry upon or remaining on property with the statutorily specified intent. Mere entry or a civil boundary dispute does not automatically establish the criminal mental element.

## 2. Documents, forgery and false records

Forgery-related analysis asks whether a false document/electronic record was made as defined by the statute, whether the accused made it with the required intent, and whether the alleged use/possession/cheating-related purpose satisfies the separate offence charged. An inaccurate document is not automatically a “false document” in the technical criminal-law sense. Separate:
- making a false document/record;
- forgery with a specified purpose;
- using a forged document as genuine;
- falsification of accounts or related special offences.

Electronic records require attention to the statutory wording and relevant evidence rules under BSA. Do not assume that every digital alteration satisfies every element of forgery.

## 3. Offences against the State and public tranquillity

BNS includes offences against the State and public tranquillity. Read the exact statutory provisions and their constitutional context. For an alleged speech/public-order offence, identify the prohibited act and mental element, assess context and likely effect, and consider freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a) alongside constitutionally permissible restrictions under Article 19(2). Do not substitute political disagreement or offensive expression for the elements of an offence.

### Unlawful assembly and rioting
Check the statutory definition of unlawful assembly, the common object, membership and the circumstances in which a member may be liable for an offence committed in prosecution of that object or one known to be likely. Rioting adds the statutory element of force or violence by the assembly or a member in prosecution of its object. Distinguish individual liability, common intention and common object.

## 4. Defamation

Defamation concerns making or publishing an imputation about a person with the legally required intent/knowledge and subject to statutory exceptions. Examine:
- the imputation and the person identified;
- publication/communication to a third party where required;
- reputational tendency and the required mental state;
- truth/public good, opinion on public conduct, fair comment or other statutory exceptions, as applicable;
- whether criminal defamation or a civil remedy is being discussed.

Truth is not a universal answer to every criminal-defamation problem: the statutory exception and its conditions matter. Criticism of public conduct must be analysed under the precise exception and constitutional context. Avoid stating that every false statement is criminal defamation.

## 6. Leading cases and how to use them

- *K.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan* (1957): dishonest intention and movement in theft under the former IPC; verify the corresponding BNS wording.
- *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar* (2000): distinguishes cheating from a mere breach of contract; dishonest intention at inception is significant.
- *R.K. Dalmia v Delhi Administration* (1962): discusses property and criminal breach of trust in its statutory context.
- *Subramanian Swamy v Union of India* (2016): upheld the constitutionality of criminal defamation under the then IPC, while discussing reputation and free speech. Read the judgment for its exact reasoning and later context.
- *Kedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar* (1962): historically important constitutional construction of sedition under the IPC; do not assume the IPC offence was simply renumbered in BNS. Analyse the present BNS provision and constitutional position separately.

These cases predate the BNS and interpret earlier provisions or constitutional issues. Use them as principles only where the statutory language and later law support the analogy.

## 7. Model answer plan: “Distinguish theft, extortion and robbery”

Start with the BNS definitions; compare the nature of taking, victim's role, fear/force and immediacy; explain robbery as an aggravated statutory category; apply examples; cite the relevant sections from the current Act; conclude by matching each fact pattern to its ingredients.

## 8. Problem method

For property and public-order scenarios:
1. identify the precise offence and any alternative offence;
2. set out the current BNS ingredients;
3. establish the accused's mental state and the relevant act;
4. distinguish civil wrong/breach of contract from crime;
5. test statutory exceptions and constitutional context where relevant;
6. state the likely result and punishment only after checking the provision.
