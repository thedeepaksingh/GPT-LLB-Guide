# Paper 4 — Mock Examination, Model Answers and Final Revision

**Paper:** Law of Crimes I — BNS/K-1006  
**Use:** Practice under the duration and choice pattern announced for your own sitting. This is a self-created practice paper, not an official CCSU paper or prediction.

## Section A — Short answers (10 × 2 = 20 marks)

Answer each in about 40–70 words.

1. Define crime and explain the principle of legality.
2. Distinguish actus reus and mens rea.
3. Distinguish preparation and attempt.
4. What is strict liability?
5. Define abetment.
6. What is the central ingredient of criminal conspiracy?
7. Distinguish culpable homicide and murder.
8. Distinguish wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement.
9. What is entrustment in criminal breach of trust?
10. State the distinction between theft and extortion.

### Model answer points — Section A

1. **Crime and legality:** Crime is conduct prohibited by criminal law and punishable by the State. Legality requires a defined offence and lawful penalty; Constitution art. 20(1) bars retrospective conviction or a greater retrospective penalty.
2. **Actus reus / mens rea:** Actus reus covers prohibited conduct, circumstances and consequence; mens rea is the fault element required by the offence. Identify the precise statutory ingredients.
3. **Preparation / attempt:** Preparation arranges means; attempt involves conduct sufficiently advanced toward commission with the required mental state. Apply BNS s. 62 and any special provision.
4. **Strict liability:** Liability for which the statute dispenses with, or materially limits, proof of a conventional mens rea element. It is not a blanket presumption; construe the exact statutory scheme.
5. **Abetment:** Statutorily defined participation by instigation, qualifying conspiracy or intentional aid; prove the mode of abetment and required mental element.
6. **Conspiracy:** Agreement between at least two persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means; apply BNS s. 61 and its overt-act rule where relevant.
7. **Homicide / murder:** Murder is the aggravated statutory category of culpable homicide. Apply the BNS mental-state/circumstance tests and any exception; do not rely on labels alone.
8. **Restraint / confinement:** Restraint obstructs a person's movement in a direction in which they have a right to proceed; confinement is wrongful restraint within circumscribed limits. Use BNS ss. 126–127.
9. **Entrustment:** Property or dominion over property is entrusted to the accused; dishonest misappropriation/conversion/use/disposal contrary to law or the relevant direction is then assessed under BNS s. 316.
10. **Theft / extortion:** Theft involves dishonest moving of movable property out of another's possession without consent; extortion involves intentionally putting a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly inducing delivery of property/value. Check ss. 303 and 308.

## Section B — Medium answers (answer any 4 × 5 = 20 marks)

### Q1. Explain the general ingredients of criminal liability.
**Model answer:** Criminal liability is determined by the exact offence provision. The court identifies (1) prohibited act or legally relevant omission, (2) circumstances specified by the statute, (3) a prohibited consequence where required, (4) causation, and (5) the required mental state such as intention, knowledge, rashness or negligence. It then considers general exceptions and offence-specific defences. The prosecution must prove the offence beyond reasonable doubt; the accused may rely on an exception under the applicable evidentiary rule. Mens rea is ordinarily important, but the statute can expressly or by necessary implication alter the rule. Article 20(1) protects legality and non-retroactivity. Apply each element separately rather than concluding that harmful conduct automatically equals a crime.

### Q2. Explain mistake of fact and accident.
**Model answer:** Mistake-of-fact protection is conditional on the statutory requirements, including the relevant good-faith belief and circumstances; mistake of law is not ordinarily a defence. Accident requires analysis of whether the act occurred while doing a lawful act in a lawful manner, by lawful means, with the required care and without the fault specified by the provision. A person cannot invoke “accident” merely because the result was unintended. Identify the exact BNS section, state each condition, apply the evidence and distinguish absence of intent from absence of negligence.

### Q3. Explain private defence and its limits.
**Model answer:** Private defence protects body or property from qualifying unlawful threats. It is preventive rather than retaliatory. Under BNS ss. 34–44, the availability and extent of the right depend on the type of threat, its imminence, statutory circumstances, when the right begins and ends, and whether the law permits force causing death in the specific situation. The court considers the facts as they appeared at the time but must apply the statutory limits. Once the threat ends, revenge is not private defence. Conclude by applying the particular provision to necessity, threat and the force used.

### Q4. Distinguish common intention and common object.
**Model answer:** Common intention under BNS s. 3(5) concerns shared intention and participation that attract joint liability. Common object operates through unlawful-assembly provisions, beginning with s. 189 and the liability rule in s. 190. An unlawful assembly requires five or more persons and a statutory common object; liability depends on the statutory connection between the offence and the assembly's object/knowledge. The doctrines have different ingredients. Neither should be inferred from mere presence without examining conduct and circumstances.

### Q5. Distinguish cheating and criminal breach of trust.
**Model answer:** Cheating under BNS s. 318 centres on deception and the statutory form of inducement; dishonest intention at the relevant time is critical. Criminal breach of trust under s. 316 requires entrustment or dominion over property and dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to the relevant legal direction or arrangement. A simple breach of contract is not automatically either offence. Identify the representation and inducement for cheating; identify entrustment and subsequent dishonest dealing for breach of trust.

### Q6. Explain dowry death and its elements.
**Model answer:** Begin with BNS s. 80 and quote its current wording. Identify the death circumstances and statutory time period, then establish cruelty or harassment connected with dowry demand “soon before” death as the provision requires. Keep substantive offence elements distinct from evidentiary presumptions, which must be checked under the applicable evidence statute and facts. Do not infer guilt merely from the fact of a marriage-related death. State each element and apply evidence to it.

## Section C — Long answers (answer any 2 × 15 = 30 marks)

### Q1. Discuss the stages of crime and the distinction between preparation and attempt.
**Model answer:** The traditional stages are intention, preparation, attempt and completion. Intention is a mental decision and is ordinarily not punishable by itself. Preparation involves arranging means or planning; it is generally not punishable unless a statute creates a specific preparatory offence. Attempt arises when the accused has the required mental state and performs acts sufficiently advanced toward commission of the intended offence. Completion occurs once all statutory elements are satisfied.

The distinction is fact-sensitive. The court considers the intended offence, the accused's purpose, the acts already done, proximity to completion, whether the conduct has moved beyond planning, and any special provision. BNS s. 62 supplies the general attempt rule, subject to offence-specific provisions. *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* and *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* are leading former-IPC authorities on attempt; use them for principle, then apply the current BNS wording. Conclude by explaining why the accused's actual acts—not intention alone or the label used by a witness—satisfy or fail the attempt test.

### Q2. Explain general exceptions and the right of private defence under BNS.
**Model answer:** General exceptions prevent criminal liability where statutory conditions justify or excuse conduct or negate an element. BNS ss. 14–44 cover, among other matters, acts under legal authority, mistake of fact, accident, necessity, infancy, unsoundness of mind, intoxication, consent-related rules, compulsion, trivial harm and private defence. The exact rule must be taken from its text; exceptions are not interchangeable.

For each exception, state its conditions and apply them to the evidence. Infancy distinguishes a child below seven from an older child within the statutory maturity rule. Unsoundness of mind concerns capacity at the time of the act, not a diagnosis alone. Intoxication requires distinction between involuntary and voluntary intoxication. Private defence is preventive, limited by statutory circumstances, and cannot justify revenge. Under ss. 34–44, examine the threat, imminence, commencement/continuation of the right, the type of protected interest, and whether the specific provision permits the degree of force used. A complete answer ends with a reasoned application and a clear conclusion on whether the exception is made out.

### Q3. Compare culpable homicide, murder and causing death by rash or negligent act.
**Model answer:** Begin with proof of death and causation. Then identify the mental state required by the relevant BNS provision. Culpable homicide and murder are governed by ss. 100–105; murder is the aggravated category determined by statutory intention/knowledge and circumstances, subject to exceptions. Causing death by rash or negligent act is a distinct route under s. 106 and requires the statutory degree of rashness/negligence and causation rather than the murder mental state. Do not treat every fatal accident as murder, or every unintended death as negligence.

Apply the injury, weapon, force, body part, prior conduct, words, medical evidence and surrounding circumstances without replacing the statutory test with any single fact. *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya* explains the conceptual relationship under the former IPC; *Virsa Singh v State of Punjab* is relevant to a specific injury-based murder principle under the old text. Both must be checked against the current BNS wording. Verify the current commencement position of s. 106(2) before discussing it. End by classifying the facts provisionally and identifying any exception or evidentiary uncertainty.

## Section D — Problem questions (answer any 2 × 15 = 30 marks)

### Problem 1 — Attempt
A, intending to steal a valuable necklace from a locked house, enters the property at night carrying tools. Before touching the necklace, A is seen by a guard and runs away. Discuss possible liability.
**Model answer:** The facts establish suspicious preparation and entry, but attempt to commit theft depends on the exact conduct and the applicable provisions. Identify whether A has merely arranged tools and entered, or has begun an act sufficiently proximate to taking movable property with dishonest intention. Consider any independently completed offence, including criminal trespass or house-breaking provisions if their ingredients are established. Apply BNS s. 62 to attempt and the specific property provisions to completed conduct. Do not infer completed theft because A intended to steal. Conclude separately on attempt and any completed offence; state what further facts are needed (entry method, location, acts directed at the necklace, ownership/possession and evidence of intent).

### Problem 2 — Private defence
B is attacked with a metal rod. During the attack B strikes the attacker once with a nearby object, causing serious injury. The attacker falls and stops attacking, but B then continues striking several times.
**Model answer:** Separate the first strike from the later blows. The first may fall within private defence if the threat was imminent and the response fits BNS ss. 34–44. Once the attacker fell and the threat ceased, continued blows may be retaliation rather than defence. The court must assess the circumstances, injury, ability to retreat only where legally relevant, the precise statutory conditions and whether the right had ended. Do not decide the whole event with one label; liability may differ by phase. The evidence about the attack, timing, force and whether the attacker remained dangerous is material.

### Problem 3 — Cheating or breach of contract
C takes an advance payment after promising to deliver machinery. At the time of the promise, C genuinely intends to deliver, but a later supplier failure prevents delivery and C does not refund the money promptly.
**Model answer:** Non-delivery alone does not establish cheating. Under BNS s. 318, ask whether deception and the required dishonest/fraudulent inducement existed at the relevant time. The facts suggest an initially genuine intention, which weakens the cheating allegation, though later conduct may be evidence and should be examined. Contractual remedies may arise independently. Criminal breach of trust requires entrustment/dominion and dishonest dealing under s. 316; an advance payment does not automatically satisfy it. Conclude that further evidence about the original representation, use of money, communications and subsequent conduct is required.

### Problem 4 — Unlawful assembly
Six people gather outside a shop. Four throw stones and damage the shutters; the other two remain nearby but shout encouragement. Discuss group liability.
**Model answer:** Determine whether five or more persons shared a statutory common object under BNS s. 189. Then apply s. 190 to whether the charged offence was committed in prosecution of that common object or was one the members knew was likely in the statutory sense. Individual presence alone is not enough; shouting encouragement, coordinated conduct, prior communications and conduct during the event may support an inference of membership and shared object. Property damage may separately satisfy a property offence if its ingredients are proved. Distinguish unlawful assembly, rioting and the underlying offence; avoid assuming all six are automatically guilty of every act.
