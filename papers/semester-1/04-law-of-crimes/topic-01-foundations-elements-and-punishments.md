# Topic 1 — Foundations, Elements, Punishments and Stages of Crime

**Paper:** CCSU LL.B. Semester I · K-1006 · Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023  
**Study note updated:** 2026-10-30 00:05 IST

## 1. Meaning and purpose of criminal law

A crime is a public wrong prohibited by law and ordinarily prosecuted by the State. Criminal law protects life, bodily integrity, liberty, property, public order and other legally recognised interests. Conviction may result in punishment, but criminal liability is constrained by legality, fair trial, proof beyond reasonable doubt and constitutional rights.

Distinguish:
- **Crime:** public wrong prosecuted under criminal law.
- **Tort:** civil wrong usually remedied by damages or injunction.
- **Breach of contract:** failure to perform a contractual obligation; it is not automatically a crime.
- One act may create criminal, civil and regulatory consequences simultaneously.

## 2. Legality and the BNS

BNS sections 1–3 contain preliminary provisions, definitions and general explanations. The principle *nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege* means no crime or punishment without law. Article 20(1) of the Constitution bars conviction for an act that was not an offence when done and bars a penalty greater than the one then applicable. Penal statutes are not ordinarily enlarged by analogy against an accused.

The BNS came into force on 1 July 2024, subject to the notified exception concerning section 106(2). For transitional questions, distinguish the date of conduct from the date of FIR, investigation or trial. The repeal-and-savings provision and applicable transition rules must be read alongside the General Clauses Act and relevant notifications. Do not assume the new Act governs conduct retrospectively.

## 3. Core elements of criminal liability

The prosecution generally must establish the following, as required by the particular offence:

1. **Human conduct:** an act or legally relevant omission. An omission normally attracts liability only where a legal duty to act exists.
2. **Prohibited consequence or circumstance:** for result crimes, the specified harm must occur; conduct crimes may be complete without a further result.
3. **Causation:** where a result is an element, the accused's conduct must have the legally required causal connection to it.
4. **Mens rea:** a required mental state such as intention, knowledge, recklessness or negligence. The exact mental element is offence-specific.
5. **Concurrence:** the required mental state must accompany the relevant conduct, subject to doctrines dealing with a continuing act or connected sequence.
6. **Absence of a valid defence:** general exceptions may negate liability even when the external elements appear present.

The maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea* (“an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty”) is a general principle, not an absolute rule. Statutory offences may impose strict or modified liability; interpret the text and legislative purpose.

## 4. Actus reus and mens rea

**Actus reus** means the external elements: conduct, circumstances and, where required, consequence. **Mens rea** means the fault element prescribed by the offence. Intention is not identical to motive: motive explains why a person acted; intention concerns the result or circumstance the person meant to bring about. Knowledge means awareness; negligence concerns a failure to meet the legally required standard of care.

Illustration: A intentionally strikes B with a heavy iron rod at the head. The physical act, injury, causation and mental state must each be assessed. The final offence depends on the injury, circumstances, statutory ingredients and any defence—not merely on the fact that a blow occurred.

## 5. Causation

For result offences, ask:
- Was the act a factual cause of the result?
- Was the result sufficiently connected to the conduct in law?
- Did an independent intervening event break the chain?
- Was the victim's vulnerability or ordinary medical treatment part of the causal sequence?

The accused need not always be the sole cause. A victim's pre-existing condition does not automatically absolve the accused if the conduct legally caused or accelerated the prohibited harm. These are application questions; the offence's statutory wording remains controlling.

## 6. Stages of crime

1. **Intention:** a mental decision alone is ordinarily not punishable.
2. **Preparation:** arranging means or planning. Usually not punishable unless the statute specifically criminalises the preparatory conduct.
3. **Attempt:** conduct sufficiently proximate to commission and beyond mere preparation, with the required intent/knowledge. BNS section 62 is a general attempt provision, subject to specific attempt offences.
4. **Commission:** all elements of the substantive offence are complete.

The preparation/attempt boundary is fact-sensitive. Relevant authorities include *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961) and *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980), which explain that attempt is assessed by proximity and the acts done toward the offence, not by a single mechanical test. Always check the precise offence-specific provision.

## 7. Punishments under BNS

BNS section 4 lists punishments, including death, imprisonment for life, imprisonment (rigorous or simple), forfeiture of property, fine and community service. The exact availability and limits depend on the offence and applicable constitutional/statutory restrictions. Sections 5–13 address commutation, fractions of terms, character of imprisonment, fines and related rules.

**Sentencing principles:** proportionality, statutory limits, relevant aggravating/mitigating circumstances, consistency, individualised sentencing and reasoned judicial discretion. Death penalty is constitutionally restricted to the “rarest of rare” category under *Bachan Singh v State of Punjab* (1980); later cases require a meaningful sentencing hearing and consideration of mitigation. Do not write that a court may choose any punishment regardless of the section.

## 8. Burden and standard of proof

The prosecution ordinarily bears the burden of proving every essential ingredient beyond reasonable doubt. The accused may rely on reasonable doubt and on statutory exceptions. Under the evidence law, an accused relying on a general exception may bear a burden of establishing circumstances bringing the case within it, ordinarily on a preponderance standard; the prosecution's ultimate burden to prove the offence remains distinct. Evidence questions should be answered under the BSA where applicable, not by treating BNS as a procedural/evidence code.

## 9. Leading cases to revise

- *R v Tolson* (1889): classic illustration of mistake of fact and the role of honest belief in a statutory context; use cautiously because modern Indian statutory text governs.
- *Nathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1966): mens rea is ordinarily presumed relevant unless excluded by statute or necessary implication.
- *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980): attempt and proximity to commission.
- *Bachan Singh v State of Punjab* (1980): death penalty limited by the rarest-of-rare doctrine.
- *Machhi Singh v State of Punjab* (1983): elaboration of sentencing considerations within the *Bachan Singh* framework.

## 10. Exam questions and answer plan

**Q. Explain the essential elements of a crime.** Define crime; discuss conduct/omission, circumstances, consequence, causation and mens rea; explain concurrence and defences; illustrate; conclude that elements are offence-specific.

**Q. Distinguish intention, knowledge, motive and negligence.** Define each; use contrasting examples; explain the statutory mental element; avoid equating motive with intention.

**Q. Distinguish preparation from attempt.** State stages; explain proximity and unequivocal acts; cite *Abhayanand Mishra* and *Mohd. Yakub*; apply to the facts; mention that specific provisions may alter the general rule.

**Q. Discuss punishments under the BNS.** Start with section 4, explain sentencing framework and statutory limits, discuss proportionality and rarest-of-rare doctrine, then conclude that punishment must remain within the offence provision and constitutional safeguards.
