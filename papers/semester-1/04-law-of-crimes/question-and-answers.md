# Paper 4 — Law of Crimes I (BNS, 2023): Master Questions and Answers

**CCSU LL.B. Semester I · K-1006 · Regular cohort 2024–25 onward**  
**Updated:** 2026-10-30 00:05 IST (Asia/Kolkata)

> This master file combines short answers, long-form exam answers, comparisons and problem questions. It follows BNS, not the legacy IPC syllabus. Before an exam, verify every section number and punishment in the official Act and compare the coverage with IEC's current unit-wise syllabus. IPC decisions are identified as historical interpretive authorities, not as a substitute for BNS text.

## Part A — Short-answer questions

### Q1. What is a crime?
**Answer:** A crime is a public wrong prohibited by law and ordinarily prosecuted by the State. It protects legally recognised interests such as life, bodily integrity, liberty, property and public order. Criminal liability requires proof of the elements of a defined offence and remains subject to constitutional safeguards and statutory defences. One act may produce criminal and civil consequences, but a civil wrong or breach of contract is not automatically a crime.

### Q2. What is the principle of legality?
**Answer:** The principle *nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege* means there can be no crime or punishment without law. Article 20(1) of the Constitution prohibits retrospective criminalisation and a greater retrospective penalty. Penal statutes are interpreted according to their text and are not ordinarily extended by analogy against an accused. For BNS questions, determine when the conduct occurred and check commencement and savings provisions.

### Q3. Explain actus reus and mens rea.
**Answer:** *Actus reus* describes the external elements of an offence—conduct, relevant circumstances and any prohibited consequence. *Mens rea* is the fault element required by the offence, such as intention, knowledge or negligence. The prosecution must establish the required elements, subject to statutory rules. Neither the presence of harm nor a bad motive alone proves every element of a particular crime.

### Q4. Distinguish intention and motive.
**Answer:** Intention concerns the result or circumstance the accused means to bring about; motive is the reason that prompts the conduct. A person may intend to cause injury for different motives. Motive can be relevant evidence, especially where the case is circumstantial, but it is not a substitute for proving the statutory mental element.

### Q5. What is criminal negligence?
**Answer:** Criminal negligence is conduct meeting the level of negligence or rashness required by the particular penal provision. It is not established merely because harm occurred or a civil duty was breached. The court examines the statutory standard, foreseeability, the conduct, causation and the surrounding circumstances.

### Q6. What is the difference between preparation and attempt?
**Answer:** Preparation consists of arranging means or planning; it is generally not punishable unless a statute expressly criminalises it. Attempt requires the necessary mental state and an act that progresses sufficiently beyond preparation toward commission. The line depends on the offence and facts. *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961) and *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980) are leading authorities on attempt under the earlier code; current BNS wording controls.

### Q7. List punishments under BNS.
**Answer:** BNS section 4 lists death, imprisonment for life, imprisonment (rigorous or simple), forfeiture of property, fine and community service. The punishment for a particular offence must be taken from its own provision and is constrained by sentencing principles and constitutional law.

### Q8. What is a general exception?
**Answer:** A general exception is a statutory circumstance that may exclude criminal liability even where conduct initially appears to satisfy an offence. BNS sections 14–44 contain major general exceptions and private-defence provisions. The accused must identify the relevant rule and establish its conditions on the applicable standard; the prosecution's burden to prove the offence remains distinct.

### Q9. What is the right of private defence?
**Answer:** Private defence is a preventive right to protect body or property against qualifying unlawful threats. It is not a right of revenge. The availability and extent of force depend on imminence, necessity, the nature of the threat, statutory conditions and limits. BNS sections 34–44 are the key range to consult.

### Q10. Define abetment.
**Answer:** Under the BNS framework, abetment can occur by instigation, by a legally relevant conspiracy coupled with the act/omission required by the provision, or by intentional aid. Mere presence or association does not automatically prove abetment. The exact provision and punishment depend on the offence abetted and statutory circumstances.

### Q11. Define criminal conspiracy.
**Answer:** BNS section 61 addresses criminal conspiracy. Its essential idea is an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. Depending on the statutory object, an overt act in pursuance may be required. Circumstantial evidence can prove an agreement, but suspicion or association alone is insufficient.

### Q12. Distinguish culpable homicide and murder.
**Answer:** Both concern unlawful killing, but murder is the aggravated category defined by the statutory mental-state and circumstance tests, subject to exceptions. The court examines causation, intention/knowledge, the nature and location of injury, surrounding facts and any exception. *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya* (1976) explains the conceptual relationship under the former IPC; the current BNS provisions must be applied directly.

### Q13. What is criminal breach of trust?
**Answer:** It generally requires entrustment of property or dominion over it and dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to the relevant legal direction or contract. Entrustment is a key distinguishing element. The facts must satisfy the BNS definition; mere non-payment or contractual default is not automatically criminal breach of trust.

### Q14. When does cheating differ from breach of contract?
**Answer:** Cheating requires deception and the statutory form of dishonest/fraudulent inducement. A later failure to perform a promise does not, by itself, prove that the promisor had dishonest intention at the outset. *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar* (2000) is a leading authority for this distinction under the earlier code.

### Q15. Is every false statement criminal defamation?
**Answer:** No. The prosecution must prove the statutory ingredients, including the relevant imputation, publication/communication where required, and mental element. Statutory exceptions may apply. A false statement does not automatically establish criminal defamation; the current BNS provision and facts must be examined.

## Part B — Long-form exam answers

### Q16. Explain the essential elements of criminal liability.
**Answer:** Criminal liability is statutory: a person may be convicted only where the prosecution proves the ingredients of a defined offence. First, there must be prohibited conduct, including an act or a legally relevant omission where a duty exists. Secondly, the offence may require specified circumstances or a prohibited consequence. Thirdly, where a result is an element, causation must connect the conduct with that result. Fourthly, the accused must possess the mental state prescribed by the offence—intention, knowledge, negligence or another specified state—unless the statute validly excludes or modifies that requirement. The relevant act and mental state must ordinarily coincide. Finally, a general exception or defence may negate liability.

The maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea* expresses the general importance of fault, but it is not absolute; statutory text controls. Article 20(1) of the Constitution reinforces legality and bars retrospective criminalisation. In *Nathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1966), the Supreme Court treated mens rea as ordinarily relevant unless excluded expressly or by necessary implication. The correct method is therefore to identify the exact BNS offence, set out each ingredient, evaluate evidence element by element, consider defences and conclude only when the statutory test is met.

### Q17. Discuss the stages of crime and the law of attempt.
**Answer:** The traditional stages are intention, preparation, attempt and completion. Intention alone is ordinarily not punishable because criminal law does not generally punish an unexecuted thought. Preparation involves arranging means or planning; it is normally not punishable unless the statute specifically criminalises it. Attempt begins when the accused, with the required mental state, performs conduct sufficiently proximate to commission and beyond mere preparation. Completion occurs when all elements of the substantive offence are fulfilled.

The distinction is practical and fact-sensitive. In *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar* (1961), the Supreme Court considered whether the acts had crossed preparation into attempt. *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* (1980) emphasised the acts done and their proximity to the intended offence. Neither case creates a mechanical test for every offence. For the current paper, BNS section 62 provides the general attempt framework, subject to offence-specific provisions. A good answer identifies the intended offence, required mental state, acts already performed, proximity, any special provision and the resulting liability.

### Q18. Explain the punishments recognised by BNS and sentencing principles.
**Answer:** BNS section 4 recognises death, imprisonment for life, imprisonment (rigorous or simple), forfeiture of property, fine and community service. Sections 5–13 contain related rules concerning commutation, fractions of terms, character of imprisonment, fines and other sentencing matters. The punishment for a particular offence is governed by its specific section; a court cannot ignore statutory limits.

Sentencing must be proportionate and individualised, taking account of the offence, harm, culpability, aggravating and mitigating circumstances, relevant precedent and constitutional requirements. In *Bachan Singh v State of Punjab* (1980), the Supreme Court restricted the death penalty to the “rarest of rare” cases. The principle requires a reasoned evaluation rather than an automatic response to public outrage. The conclusion should state the available statutory range and explain the factors relevant to selecting a sentence, without predicting an outcome unsupported by the facts.

### Q19. Discuss the general exceptions under BNS.
**Answer:** General exceptions prevent punishment where the statutory conditions justify or excuse conduct or negate a required element. BNS sections 14–44 cover important rules including acts connected with legal authority, mistake of fact in specified circumstances, accident, necessity, infancy, unsoundness of mind, intoxication, consent-related rules, compulsion, trivial harm and private defence. The exact conditions vary and must be taken from the text.

A mistake of fact may be relevant where the statute protects an honest belief under specified conditions; ignorance of law is not generally an excuse. Accident requires attention to lawfulness and due care. Infancy rules distinguish children below seven from children above seven and below twelve who lack sufficient maturity. The unsoundness-of-mind exception concerns legal capacity at the time, not merely a diagnosis. Intoxication must be analysed as voluntary or involuntary under the statutory rule. Consent is limited by capacity, coercion, age and the nature of harm. Private defence is preventive and bounded by necessity and statutory limits. The answer should identify the particular exception, apply each condition to the evidence and conclude whether it is available.

### Q20. Explain the right of private defence of body and property.
**Answer:** Private defence recognises that a person may need to protect body or property against an unlawful threat before public authorities can intervene. The right is preventive rather than punitive. BNS sections 34–44 provide the statutory framework; the exact circumstances in which force, including force causing death, is permitted must be taken from the relevant provisions.

The court considers the nature and imminence of the threat, necessity of defensive action, the harm reasonably faced, the response used, whether public protection could be sought, and whether the threat had ended. The law does not require a person under attack to calculate force with mathematical precision, but it does not authorise revenge or unnecessary injury. In *Darshan Singh v State of Punjab* (2010), the Supreme Court explained core principles of private defence. A complete answer distinguishes defence from retaliation, applies the facts to the statutory limits and avoids assuming that the mere fact of injury either establishes or defeats the defence.

### Q21. Explain abetment and distinguish it from conspiracy.
**Answer:** Abetment under the BNS may be committed through instigation, a statutorily defined form of conspiracy with the required act/omission, or intentional aid. The mental element and mode of participation are essential; mere presence or association is not enough. Criminal conspiracy under BNS section 61 focuses on an agreement to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means, with the statutory rules governing when an overt act is required.

The doctrines overlap but are not identical. Conspiracy centres on agreement; abetment may exist through instigation or intentional aid without proof of a separate conspiracy offence. Conspiracy is often proved by circumstances, yet the circumstances must establish a meeting of minds rather than mere suspicion. *Kehar Singh v State (Delhi Administration)* (1988) discusses circumstantial proof of conspiracy. The answer should identify the charged provision, the accused's role, evidence of agreement or aid, and the relevant statutory consequence.

### Q22. Distinguish common intention and common object.
**Answer:** Common intention concerns shared intention and the statutory requirements for joint liability; it may be inferred from conduct and circumstances and need not always arise long before the event. Common object concerns liability connected with membership of an unlawful assembly and the statutory object/knowledge rules. They have different ingredients and should not be used interchangeably. In *Pandurang, Tukia and Bhillia v State of Hyderabad* (1955), the Supreme Court explained common intention under the former IPC. For a BNS answer, cite the current provision, prove the relevant participation and mental element, and assess each accused's role. Mere presence at a scene is not automatically sufficient.

### Q23. Explain the distinction between culpable homicide and murder.
**Answer:** The distinction turns on the statutory mental-state and circumstance tests, together with applicable exceptions. The court first establishes death and causation. It then evaluates whether the accused intended death, intended the relevant kind of bodily injury, or had the knowledge specified by the provision. It must apply the statutory definition of murder and consider whether an exception reduces the offence to culpable homicide not amounting to murder. The nature and location of the injury, weapon, force, prior conduct and surrounding circumstances may be relevant, but no single fact automatically decides the case.

*Virsa Singh v State of Punjab* (1958) explains a limb of the murder analysis under the former IPC; *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya* (1976) explains the relationship between culpable homicide and murder. Because those decisions interpret earlier wording, a BNS answer must compare the current statutory text before applying their reasoning. Conclude by matching proved facts to the current offence rather than simply labelling every death murder.

### Q24. Explain the ingredients of theft, extortion and robbery.
**Answer:** Theft generally concerns dishonest taking/movement of movable property from another's possession without consent as defined by BNS. Extortion centres on intentionally putting a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly inducing delivery of property or another statutorily specified thing. Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion when the additional statutory conditions concerning violence, attempted violence or fear of instant harm are met. Dacoity involves the specific statutory group threshold and connected conditions.

The differences lie in the manner of acquisition, the role of the victim's delivery, the use or threat of force and the statutory aggravation. The answer should quote or accurately paraphrase the current BNS definitions and identify each element. It should not rely on IPC section numbers or assume every forceful taking satisfies robbery without checking the exact statutory conditions.

### Q25. Explain cheating and distinguish it from a mere breach of contract.
**Answer:** Cheating requires deception and the dishonest or fraudulent inducement specified by the BNS provision, causing or likely to cause the relevant delivery, retention, act, omission or harm. A contract may be broken because of later inability, changed circumstances or poor performance without the initial promise having been dishonest. The critical issue is whether the required dishonest intention existed at the relevant time and whether the remaining statutory elements are satisfied.

In *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar* (2000), the Supreme Court emphasised the distinction between cheating and a mere breach of contract under the former code. The decision is useful for the principle, but the current BNS text governs. Apply the contemporaneous representations, conduct and evidence, and do not infer criminality solely from non-performance.

### Q26. Discuss criminal defamation and its limits.
**Answer:** Criminal defamation requires proof of the imputation, the statutory mental element and publication or communication as required by the provision, subject to statutory exceptions. The analysis must distinguish a factual imputation from opinion, identify the person concerned and consider the context and reputational effect. Statutory exceptions may address truth in specified circumstances, public conduct, fair criticism or other protected categories; their exact conditions matter. Truth should not be described as an unconditional defence without checking the provision.

In *Subramanian Swamy v Union of India* (2016), the Supreme Court upheld criminal defamation under the then IPC framework and considered reputation alongside freedom of speech. A current answer should analyse the BNS wording and constitutional setting. Not every false or offensive statement is automatically criminal defamation.

## Part C — Comparison questions

### Q27. Distinguish criminal negligence and intentional homicide.
**Answer:** Intentional homicide requires the mental state specified by the homicide provisions; negligence-based causing of death requires the form and degree of negligence or rashness prescribed by its provision. Both require the relevant causal link, but the mental elements differ. A fatal result does not itself prove intention, and describing conduct as an accident does not automatically exclude criminal negligence. Apply the exact BNS sections, evidence and any commencement exception relevant to section 106(2).

### Q28. Distinguish criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust.
**Answer:** Criminal misappropriation centres on dishonest misappropriation or conversion of property. Criminal breach of trust additionally requires entrustment or dominion over property and dishonest dealing contrary to the relevant legal direction or contract. The presence or absence of entrustment is often decisive. The facts must meet the statutory elements; a civil accounting dispute alone does not establish criminal liability.

### Q29. Distinguish unlawful assembly and rioting.
**Answer:** Unlawful assembly depends on the statutory number of persons, common object and other conditions. Rioting requires the additional statutory element of force or violence by the assembly or a member in prosecution of its object. Membership-based liability depends on the relevant BNS provisions and the facts concerning object/knowledge; it should not be confused with common intention.

### Q30. Distinguish substantive criminal law, criminal procedure and evidence.
**Answer:** Substantive criminal law defines offences, defences and punishments; BNS is the principal new central code in this area. Criminal procedure governs investigation, arrest, bail, trial and related process; BNSS is the new central procedural code. Evidence law governs admissibility, proof and evidentiary rules; BSA is the new central evidence statute. A single case may require all three, but a K-1006 substantive-law answer should not treat procedural or evidence provisions as if they define the offence.

## Part D — Problem questions with model application

### Q31. A plans to steal a phone, buys gloves and travels to the location, but is arrested before approaching the phone. Is attempt established?
**Answer:** Identify the intended theft and required dishonest intention. Buying gloves and travelling to the location may show preparation, but whether conduct crossed into attempt depends on the acts performed and their proximity to taking/moving the property. On these limited facts, attempt is not automatically established. Apply BNS section 62 and any specific provision, then explain what additional conduct would matter.

### Q32. A strikes B once during a sudden confrontation; B dies. Is A guilty of murder?
**Answer:** Death and causation must be proved, but a single blow does not automatically establish or exclude murder. Determine the weapon, force, body part, injury, intention/knowledge, surrounding circumstances and whether a statutory exception applies. Compare the facts to the current BNS homicide definitions and exceptions. A reasoned conclusion may be murder, culpable homicide not amounting to murder, or another offence depending on the evidence.

### Q33. A manager receives entrusted funds for a defined purpose and dishonestly diverts them for personal use. What offence may apply?
**Answer:** Consider criminal breach of trust: prove entrustment/dominion, dishonest misappropriation or conversion, and the prohibited use/disposal. Also consider any other applicable offence only if its separate ingredients are proved. A contractual relationship alone is not enough, but entrustment plus dishonest diversion may satisfy the core elements. Cite the current BNS provision after checking its wording.

### Q34. A business accepts an advance payment but later fails to deliver due to a genuine supply-chain failure. Is that cheating?
**Answer:** Non-delivery alone is not sufficient. The prosecution must prove deception and the required dishonest inducement, including the relevant intention at the time of the representation. Genuine subsequent failure may point to a civil breach, though surrounding evidence could show the original promise was dishonest. Apply *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma* as a principle and the current BNS provision.

### Q35. A group gathers for a lawful protest; one participant unexpectedly damages a nearby vehicle. Are all participants liable?
**Answer:** Do not infer group liability merely from presence at the protest. Identify whether an unlawful assembly existed, its statutory common object, whether force/violence occurred in prosecution of that object, and what each accused knew or did. An unexpected individual act may not be attributable to every participant absent the relevant statutory conditions. Analyse individual liability separately and consider the evidence.

### Q36. A uses force to stop an immediate knife attack and injures the attacker. Is the force lawful?
**Answer:** Analyse private defence under BNS sections 34–44: imminence and nature of threat, necessity of the response, extent of force legally permitted, whether the attack had ceased and any relevant statutory limit. The fact that the attacker is injured does not itself defeat the defence; nor does the claim of defence automatically justify all force. Apply *Darshan Singh* and the current statutory wording to the facts.

## Part E — Final revision and source check

### Q37. Which cases should be prioritised?
**Answer:** Revise *Nathulal* (mens rea), *Abhayanand Mishra* and *Mohd. Yakub* (attempt), *Bachan Singh* (capital sentencing), *Darshan Singh* (private defence), *Kehar Singh* (conspiracy), *Virsa Singh* and *Rayavarapu Punnayya* (homicide), *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma* (cheating vs contract) and *Subramanian Swamy* (criminal defamation). For each case, learn facts, issue, holding/ratio and the limits of applying a pre-BNS decision to current statutory wording.

### Q38. What should be checked before the examination?
**Answer:** Confirm the official K-1006 unit-wise syllabus and question pattern; read the current India Code BNS text; verify section numbers, ingredients, exceptions and punishment; check commencement/transition notifications, especially section 106(2); use current case-law citations; and separate BNS substantive law from BNSS procedure, BSA evidence and special statutes such as POCSO. Treat coaching predictions as unconfirmed.

## Suggested 10-mark answer structure

1. Definition and legal issue.
2. Current BNS section and exact ingredients.
3. Explanation of each ingredient.
4. Relevant leading authority and ratio.
5. Application/illustration.
6. Defence, exception or counterargument.
7. Reasoned conclusion and punishment framework, if asked.

## Suggested 15/20-mark answer structure

Use a short introduction, statutory framework, detailed elements, case-law discussion, critical distinction, application/example, counterargument, and conclusion. Never pad an answer with unrelated IPC sections; accuracy and application are more valuable than a long list of authorities.
