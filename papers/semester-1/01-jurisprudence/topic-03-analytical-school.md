## 1. Central concern
Analytical jurisprudence asks what law is, how legal systems identify valid law, and how concepts such as command, sovereignty, validity, duty, rule and authority function. It seeks conceptual clarity. “Analytical” does not mean that social effects or morality are unimportant to every analytical theorist; it means that analysis of law’s structure and source is a central method.

Legal positivism generally distinguishes legal validity from moral merit. It does not follow that every positivist approves of every valid rule or considers morality irrelevant to political criticism.

## 2. Jeremy Bentham — precursor and past-paper topic
Bentham’s utilitarian principle evaluates laws and institutions by their tendency to promote overall happiness or reduce suffering. He criticized obscure, arbitrary and poorly organized law and advocated systematic codification and legal reform.

Useful distinctions:
- **Expository jurisprudence:** describes what the law is.
- **Censorial jurisprudence:** evaluates what the law ought to be.
- **Utility:** supplies a standard for reform and evaluation.
- **Codification:** makes legal rules more accessible and systematically organized.

Bentham influenced analytical positivism but should not be collapsed into Austin’s command theory. His utilitarian reform program has an evaluative objective, whereas Austin’s analytical account focuses on the structure of positive law.

**Criticisms:** aggregating welfare can endanger individual rights if safeguards are absent; measuring happiness is difficult; a codified system may become rigid or fail to reflect evolving social conditions.

## 3. John Austin — Command theory
Austin’s *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) is associated with a command theory of positive law.

### Essential elements
1. **Command:** an expression of a wish by a superior that another person act or refrain from acting, coupled with the prospect of harm for disobedience.
2. **Duty:** the person to whom the command is directed is under a duty to obey.
3. **Sanction:** the threatened harm associated with disobedience.
4. **Sovereign:** a determinate human superior habitually obeyed by the bulk of the society, who does not habitually obey another determinate human superior.
5. **Positive law:** law properly so called is set by a political superior for political inferiors. Austin distinguishes it from positive morality, custom not adopted by the sovereign, and divine law.

The account connects law, command, sovereignty and sanction. It offers a clear model for identifying certain forms of state-made rules.

### Criticisms
- **Power-conferring rules:** rules governing contracts, wills, corporations or legislation do not fit neatly into a command backed by a sanction.
- **Custom:** customs can be treated as law even without a clear sovereign command or adoption at a single identifiable moment.
- **Constitutional limits:** the sovereign concept struggles to explain a constitution that legally limits officials or divides authority.
- **International law:** Austin’s treatment of international law as positive morality fails to capture modern accounts of legal obligation and institutions.
- **Continuity:** a legal system may continue despite a change in the individual or group exercising political authority.
- **Habit is not the whole legal picture:** officials and citizens often treat rules as standards that justify criticism, not merely as predictions of punishment.
- **Moral criticism:** Austin’s source-based approach can identify positive law but does not by itself establish that the law is just.

### Exam-ready balanced view
Austin’s model is important for emphasizing identifiable sources, political authority and sanctions. It is insufficient as a complete account of modern legal systems because law also contains power-conferring rules, institutional procedures, constitutional limits and practices of official recognition.

## 4. Hans Kelsen — Pure Theory of Law
Kelsen sought a “pure” legal theory that separates analysis of legal validity from moral, political, sociological or psychological explanations. The goal is methodological: to study law as a normative system without reducing legal validity to morality or raw force.

### A. Norms and the “ought”
A legal norm prescribes what ought to be done or what legal consequence follows from specified conditions. It differs from a factual statement that something regularly happens. Legal science describes norms and their relationships; it does not simply explain behaviour by fear or habit.

### B. Hierarchy of norms
A lower-level norm is valid because it is created under an authorizing higher norm. For example, an administrative regulation may depend on a statute, and the statute on constitutional rules governing legislative authority. The hierarchy concerns legal validity and competence, not simply which rule is morally better.

### C. Grundnorm (basic norm)
The hierarchy cannot be explained indefinitely by pointing to another enacted rule. Kelsen therefore uses the idea of a **presupposed basic norm (Grundnorm)** as the ultimate point of validity for interpreting a legal order as a normative system. It is not an ordinary section of a constitution, a statute personally enacted by someone, or a sanction. Do not describe it as a literal written provision.

### D. Efficacy
A legal order must be broadly effective as a whole for the system to be treated as valid in Kelsen’s theoretical account. This does not mean that every individual rule must be obeyed in every instance. Effectiveness and validity are related but are not identical: the fact that people obey a rule does not by itself establish the legal source of its validity.

### E. Dynamic character
A legal system authorizes the creation of new norms through specified procedures. Legal validity therefore depends on the way a norm is made within a normative hierarchy, not simply on the substantive wisdom of the rule.

### Strengths
- Explains relationships among constitutional, legislative and delegated norms.
- Separates legal validity from moral approval.
- Emphasizes competence and rule-governed creation of law.
- Supplies a systematic account of legal order.

### Criticisms
- The Grundnorm can appear hypothetical or difficult to justify.
- Pure norm analysis may understate social power, political conflict and actual court practice.
- A validity hierarchy alone does not explain how officials identify the relevant higher norm in contested cases.
- The theory’s relation between efficacy and validity can be difficult in revolutions or contested constitutional orders.

## 5. H.L.A. Hart — law as a union of primary and secondary rules
Hart’s *The Concept of Law* (1961) criticizes Austin’s reduction of law to commands backed by threats. Hart presents a legal system as a union of primary and secondary rules.

### A. Primary rules
These impose duties: for example, rules prohibiting certain conduct or requiring performance of obligations.

### B. Secondary rules
These are rules about identifying, changing and applying primary rules:
1. **Rule of recognition:** supplies the criteria officials use to identify valid law in a system.
2. **Rules of change:** empower people or institutions to introduce, amend or repeal rules.
3. **Rules of adjudication:** empower and guide bodies that determine whether rules have been violated and resolve disputes.

A mature legal system has not only duties but also institutional procedures for identifying and changing law and deciding disputes.

### C. Internal point of view
Officials and other participants can accept rules as standards for conduct and criticism, rather than merely predicting punishment. Hart does not require that every citizen morally approve of the legal system; the point is that a rule system cannot be adequately explained only as a habit of obedience driven by fear.

### D. Open texture
Language has clear core applications and uncertain borderline cases. General rules cannot anticipate every future factual situation, so judges sometimes exercise limited discretion when a case falls within open texture. This is not a claim that judges may decide every dispute however they wish; text, precedent, institutional role and legal principles still constrain decisions.

### E. Minimum content of natural law
Hart recognizes that certain basic rules are necessary for a viable social order given familiar facts about human beings and the world: human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources and limited understanding/will. This “minimum content” does not erase his distinction between legal validity and moral merit.

### Criticism
- Critics argue that the rule of recognition cannot always settle hard cases because officials may disagree about principles or interpretation.
- Ronald Dworkin challenged the idea that legal reasoning can be explained only through rules and discretion, emphasizing principles and rights.
- Hart’s theory may leave unresolved how a system’s moral legitimacy should be judged, although this is not its primary descriptive task.

## 6. Major comparison — Austin, Kelsen and Hart
| Question | Austin | Kelsen | Hart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic account | Commands of political sovereign backed by sanctions | Hierarchy of valid norms grounded in a presupposed Grundnorm | Union of primary and secondary rules |
| Validity test | Connection to sovereign command | Norm is created under an authorizing higher norm | Criteria in the system’s rule of recognition |
| Role of sanction | Central to the command model | Legal norms often link conditions to sanctions, but validity is normative | Not every rule is reducible to a threat; sanctions do not explain the whole system |
| Sovereignty | Central | Reframed through normative hierarchy | Critiques habit-of-obedience model |
| Power-conferring rules | Difficult fit | Explainable as norms in hierarchy | Explained through secondary rules and legal powers |
| Constitutional change | Difficult when authority is legally limited | Analysed through norms authorizing change and possible order replacement | Analysed through rules of change and recognition |
| Role of morality | Separate from positive-law identification | Not the criterion of legal validity within pure theory | Validity distinguished from morality; minimum natural-law content acknowledged |
| Key term | Sovereign command | Grundnorm | Rule of recognition |
| Main weakness | Narrow account of legal rules | Abstract/hypothetical ultimate norm | Hard cases and principles may challenge rule-based account |

## 7. Illustrative application
Suppose an agency issues a regulation:
- Austin asks whether it can be characterized through a sovereign command/authority and sanction.
- Kelsen asks which higher norm authorizes the agency and whether the regulation was made within that norm’s limits.
- Hart asks which recognized criteria establish validity and which rules empower the agency and courts.
- A natural-law critic may separately ask whether the regulation is unjust.
The questions are related but not interchangeable. One cannot decide legal validity solely from moral disapproval, and one cannot decide moral justification solely from formal validity.

## 8. Indian legal relevance
The Indian Constitution provides a useful setting for questions of hierarchy, authority and interpretation:
- *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225, illustrates debate about Parliament’s amendment power and limits on constitutional change.
- *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India*, (1978) 1 SCC 248, illustrates how courts reason about the meaning of constitutional text and procedural fairness.
These examples should not be described as cases “applying Kelsen” or “adopting Hart” without direct support. Use them to discuss the problem—hierarchy, validity, interpretation—not to attribute a theory inaccurately.

## 9. Model long answer
### Q. Explain and critically evaluate Kelsen’s Pure Theory of Law, with reference to hierarchy of norms and Grundnorm.
**Introduction.** Hans Kelsen sought a pure legal science that examines law as a normative order and distinguishes legal validity from moral, political or sociological evaluation.

**Normative system.** Legal rules prescribe what ought to occur under specified conditions. Their validity is not merely a fact about obedience or a judgment about justice.

**Hierarchy.** A norm is valid because it is created in accordance with a higher norm that authorizes its creation. Rules made by administrative bodies must comply with the authority conferred by legislation; legislation must conform to constitutional rules governing legislative power. A defect in competence or procedure may therefore affect validity.

**Grundnorm.** At the highest level, Kelsen posits a presupposed basic norm that permits the legal order to be understood as a unified normative system. The Grundnorm is not a written constitutional section or an ordinary enacted rule. It is a theoretical explanation of the ultimate presupposition of legal validity.

**Effectiveness.** The legal system must be generally effective as an order, though an individual rule need not always be obeyed. Efficacy is distinct from the validity of a specific norm.

**Merits.** The theory clarifies normative hierarchy, legal competence and the distinction between valid law and good law. It helps explain why an official’s regulation cannot be valid merely because the official has issued it.

**Criticism.** The Grundnorm can seem hypothetical; pure analysis may understate politics and social practice; and the theory may not give a simple answer when the ultimate authority of a constitutional order is contested.

**Conclusion.** Kelsen’s theory offers a powerful model of a legal system as a hierarchy of norms. Its strength is systematic explanation of legal validity; its limitation is that legal practice, moral legitimacy and political change may require further theories.

## 11. Quick recall
- Austin = **sovereign + command + duty + sanction**.
- Kelsen = **norm + hierarchy + authorizing higher norm + presupposed Grundnorm**.
- Hart = **primary rules + secondary rules + rule of recognition + internal point of view**.
- Bentham = **utility + expository/censorial jurisprudence + codification/reform**.
- Do not equate Grundnorm with a constitutional article, or Hart’s rule of recognition with a sovereign’s personal command.
