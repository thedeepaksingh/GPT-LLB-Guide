# K-1001 — Revision deck and active-recall plan
**Created:** 2026-10-27 23:16 IST  
Cover the question side before revealing the answer. For an oral recall session, answer in 30–60 seconds.

## 1. Flashcards
1. **What does jurisprudence study?** — Nature, sources, validity, concepts, purposes, interpretation and operation of law.
2. **Austin’s four key terms?** — Sovereign, command, duty, sanction.
3. **Why is Austin criticized?** — Hard to fit custom, power-conferring rules, constitutional limits and modern international law into the command model.
4. **What is Kelsen’s Grundnorm?** — A presupposed basic norm explaining the unity/ultimate validity of a normative legal order; not a written statutory section.
5. **What is Kelsen’s hierarchy?** — A lower norm is valid under an authorizing higher norm.
6. **What are Hart’s primary rules?** — Rules imposing duties.
7. **What are Hart’s three secondary-rule categories?** — Recognition, change, adjudication.
8. **What is Hart’s internal point of view?** — Participants treat rules as standards for conduct/criticism, not merely as predictions of punishment.
9. **What is natural law with variable content?** — Stammler’s attempt to retain a rational ideal of just ordering while allowing concrete legal content to change with social conditions.
10. **What is Kohler’s focus?** — Law in its relation to culture and civilization.
11. **What is Volksgeist?** — Savigny’s idea of a people’s shared historical legal consciousness.
12. **What is the status-to-contract proposition?** — Maine’s thesis that progressive societies broadly moved from inherited status-based relations toward contract-based relations.
13. **What did Savigny argue about codification?** — Warned against premature codification detached from historical/legal development; not an absolute ban on legislation.
14. **What is Pound’s social engineering?** — Identifying and reconciling competing interests through law while minimizing friction/waste.
15. **Pound’s usual interest categories?** — Individual, public, social.
16. **What is Duguit’s social solidarity?** — Social interdependence gives rise to objective social obligations and constrains public authority.
17. **What is rule skepticism?** — Rules do not always mechanically dictate one inevitable result.
18. **What is fact skepticism?** — Fact-finding, witness credibility and the trial process introduce uncertainty.
19. **Who is associated with fact skepticism?** — Jerome Frank.
20. **Who is an important realist precursor associated with a predictive/practical view?** — Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.
21. **What does “law in action” mean?** — Study of actual operation and effects of law, not only its formal statement.
22. **What is historical materialism?** — A way to analyse social development through material production and relations of production.
23. **What is the economic base/superstructure distinction?** — The material/economic organization of production and the legal/political/ideological institutions related to it.
24. **What is economic determinism?** — An overly mechanical claim that law is fully determined by economics; a simplistic account to avoid.
25. **Can a valid law be morally bad in positivism?** — Yes; validity and moral merit are conceptually distinct, though theorists vary.
26. **Does natural law have one single definition?** — No; it is a family of theories about law’s relation to reason, nature, justice or morality.
27. **Does legal realism mean judges can decide anything?** — No; it emphasizes behaviour, context and uncertainty while rules and institutions remain important.
28. **Why is a case included in a theory answer?** — To illuminate the point with a verified holding; not merely to list impressive names.
29. **What did *Puttaswamy* decide?** — The Supreme Court’s nine-judge bench recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right.
30. **What should you do with a past-paper pattern?** — Use it for practice, but verify the current exam instructions.

## 2. Seven-day revision cycle
- **Day 1:** Introduction and definitions; create one page with definitions from Austin, Holland, Salmond and Pound.
- **Day 2:** Natural law; compare classical theory, Stammler and Kohler.
- **Day 3:** Analytical school; draw Austin → Kelsen → Hart comparison without looking at notes.
- **Day 4:** Historical school; teach Savigny and Maine aloud, then write the comparison from memory.
- **Day 5:** Sociological school; map Pound’s interests and Duguit’s social solidarity.
- **Day 6:** American realism and Marxist approach; write one short essay on each.
- **Day 7:** Attempt a timed mock paper; mark it with the rubric; return to weak cards only.

This cycle is a suggested plan and can be compressed or expanded. It is not a substitute for the official timetable.

## 3. Spaced recall
Review the cards on the day studied, the next day, after three days, after one week and before the exam. On each revisit, mark:
- **Green:** answer correctly and explain why.
- **Amber:** recall the label but miss an important element.
- **Red:** cannot explain without notes.
Spend revision time mainly on amber/red concepts, not passive rereading of the green ones.

## 4. One-minute oral tests
- Explain Austin, Kelsen and Hart to a student who has never studied jurisprudence.
- Explain why legal validity and moral justification are different questions.
- Contrast natural law and positivism without caricaturing either.
- Explain how history differs from sociology as an explanation of law.
- Explain how Pound and Duguit differ.
- Explain why realism is more than cynicism about judges.
- Explain the value and limits of a Marxist economic analysis.
