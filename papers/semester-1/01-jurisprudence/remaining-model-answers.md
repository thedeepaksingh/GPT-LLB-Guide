## Ten-mark answers

### Q1. Revival of natural law through Stammler and Kohler
Natural-law theories connect law's evaluation or authority with reason, justice, human nature or standards beyond enactment. The modern revival sought to retain a critical standard for positive law while recognizing that legal rules must respond to changing social conditions.

Rudolf Stammler is associated with “natural law with variable content.” He distinguishes a rational ideal of just legal ordering from particular legal rules that vary between societies and periods. This avoids treating natural law as a fixed code, but the abstract ideal can be difficult to translate into predictable decisions.

Josef Kohler links law to culture and civilization. Legal rules should be understood in relation to the cultural life and development of society. This highlights legal context but risks cultural relativism if prevailing culture becomes the final measure of justice.

Both approaches reject the idea that legal validity alone settles every question of justice. Stammler emphasizes a rational ideal; Kohler emphasizes culture. Their weakness is uncertainty about how to identify and apply the relevant standard in hard cases. The revival adapted natural law to social change without reducing law entirely to enactment.

### Q2. Austin's command theory
John Austin describes positive law as the command of a determinate political superior to political inferiors, backed by sanctions. A command expresses a wish that another act or refrain from acting; it imposes a duty and is supported by a threatened consequence. Austin's sovereign is habitually obeyed by the bulk of society and is not habitually obedient to another earthly superior.

The theory's strengths are clarity, analytical precision and emphasis on positive law, authority and sanctions. It helped distinguish description of law from moral evaluation. Its limitations are that power-conferring rules governing contracts, wills and legislation do not fit neatly into the command model; custom may acquire legal status without a specific command; constitutional rules may limit authority; legal systems survive changes in rulers; and international law lacks a single global sovereign. Hart and Kelsen provide alternative explanations.

### Q3. Hart's theory and comparison with Austin
H.L.A. Hart explains a developed legal system as a union of primary and secondary rules. Primary rules impose duties. Secondary rules identify, change and adjudicate primary rules. The three main secondary-rule types are the rule of recognition, rules of change and rules of adjudication.

The rule of recognition supplies criteria officials use to identify valid law. It is a social rule accepted from an internal point of view, rather than merely a prediction of punishment. Hart also recognizes open texture: general language cannot anticipate every future situation, so some cases require judicial judgment.

Austin emphasizes sovereign commands and sanctions. Hart argues that commands do not adequately explain power-conferring rules, legal continuity or officials' acceptance of legal standards. Hart better explains complex institutions, although the rule of recognition may be contested in constitutional crises.

### Q4. Savigny and Volksgeist
Savigny argues that law develops organically through a people's history, custom and shared legal consciousness, termed Volksgeist. Law is not simply the arbitrary product of a single legislator; it emerges through social practice and is later systematized by jurists.

The historical school reacted partly against abstract schemes for imposing comprehensive codes without regard to social history. Savigny was skeptical of premature codification where legal science and social conditions were not ready. The theory respects continuity and warns against importing rules without understanding local institutions. Its weaknesses are romanticizing tradition, assuming a unified social spirit in a diverse society and preserving oppressive practices because they are historical. Custom must still be assessed against rights and justice.

### Q5. Maine's status-to-contract proposition
Sir Henry Maine's proposition that progressive societies move “from status to contract” describes a broad historical tendency. Status refers to rights and duties substantially fixed by inherited or assigned social position; contract refers to relations increasingly shaped by agreement.

The thesis helps explain the growth of individual legal capacity and contractual relations. It is not a universal law. Modern law retains status-based rules involving children, family, citizenship and public duties. Contractual freedom is also limited by regulation and unequal bargaining power, so formal consent may not equal substantive freedom. Maine's thesis is a useful historical lens, not a linear path followed by every society.

### Q6. Duguit's social solidarity
Léon Duguit emphasizes human interdependence and the cooperation needed for social life. Social solidarity means people depend on one another and institutions must perform functions that sustain collective life. Law is not explained only by sovereign will; public authority should be assessed by the social functions it performs.

The theory emphasizes public service, responsibility and limits on arbitrary power. Its limitation is that solidarity may not supply a precise answer when interests conflict, and a claimed collective good can threaten minority rights. Duguit differs from Austin's command model and Pound's explicit interest-balancing framework. Clear legal standards and rights safeguards remain necessary.

### Q7. Pound's social-engineering theory
Roscoe Pound treats law as a means of ordering social relations by identifying and reconciling competing individual, public and social interests, reducing friction and avoidable waste. Individual interests concern personality, domestic relations and property; public interests concern the State and public functions; social interests concern safety, institutions, resources and general welfare.

The method asks how legal rules work in practice and can guide reform. For example, workplace-safety rules reconcile business operation with worker safety and public welfare. Its strengths are practical focus and attention to consequences. Its limits are that balancing requires value judgments, powerful groups may dominate the definition of interests, and a technocratic approach may under-protect rights. It is a useful analytical method only when assumptions, evidence and safeguards are explicit.

### Q8. American legal realism
American legal realism rejects the assumption that every judicial result can be mechanically deduced from abstract rules. It studies actual legal institutions, facts, judicial behaviour and the effects of decisions. Holmes emphasized prediction of what courts will do; Llewellyn examined institutional practices and contextual decision-making; Frank highlighted uncertainty in fact-finding and credibility assessment.

Rule skepticism questions whether rules uniquely determine outcomes; fact skepticism emphasizes uncertainty in findings of fact. Realism's strength is attention to law in action. Its limitation is that prediction alone may not explain legal obligation and an exaggerated account can understate the constraints of text, precedent and professional norms. Realism does not mean judges may decide arbitrarily.

### Q9. Marxist economic approach
The Marxist approach studies law in relation to material production, property, labour, class and economic power. Historical materialism asks how economic structures shape social and legal institutions. A Marxist analysis may ask whether formally general rules stabilize existing property relations or whether legal equality coexists with unequal material resources.

Its strength is exposing the effects of economic power on lawmaking, enforcement and access to justice. It can illuminate labour, property and criminalization. It must not become crude economic determinism: legal institutions may have relative autonomy, constrain powerful actors and support reform. Not every doctrine is reducible to one class interest.

### Q10. Natural law versus legal positivism
Natural-law theories connect legal evaluation or authority to reason, justice or moral standards. Positivist theories generally identify legal validity by social sources and recognized legal criteria, distinguishing validity from moral merit. Neither tradition is uniform: natural-law thinkers differ on whether injustice affects validity or moral obligation, while positivists differ on the sources and structure of legal validity.

Positivism's strength is conceptual clarity about what the law currently is; its risk is that description may be mistaken for moral endorsement. Natural law supplies critical standards for evaluating injustice; its difficulty is disagreement about which moral principles govern. A lawyer should first identify the applicable legal rule, then separately explain any moral criticism or reform argument.

## Extension questions — model answers

### Can a law be legally valid but unjust?
Yes, under many positivist accounts, a rule can satisfy the legal system's criteria of validity while being morally objectionable. Legal validity and moral legitimacy are distinct questions. Natural-law theories differ: some argue grave injustice undermines law's claim to authority, while others distinguish validity from moral obligation. The answer should identify the specific theory rather than claim all positivists or natural-law thinkers agree.

### Does formal equality establish substantive equality?
Not necessarily. A rule can apply in identical words while operating unequally because people have different resources, bargaining power or social conditions. A Marxist approach asks how property and class shape the practical effects of law. This is a critical analytical perspective, not proof that every neutral rule serves a class interest. Evidence about the rule's design and effects is needed.

### Does social purpose justify every restriction on rights?
No. Pound's interest-balancing approach helps identify competing social needs, but constitutional rights and legal standards constrain permissible measures. A public objective does not automatically establish lawful authority, necessity or proportionality. The analysis must identify the relevant right, lawful basis, authorized restriction ground and applicable legal test.

### Does judicial uncertainty mean judicial arbitrariness?
No. Realists highlight uncertainty in rules, facts and institutional practice, but legal decision-making remains constrained by statutes, precedent, evidence, procedure, appellate review and reason-giving. Uncertainty means outcomes may not be mechanically predetermined; it does not mean every outcome is equally lawful.

## Problem-answer method for jurisprudence
For a jurisprudence problem, identify the theory the facts illustrate, define its key concept, apply it to the facts, state the strongest criticism or competing explanation, and conclude narrowly. Do not treat a theorist's descriptive account as automatically proving what the law ought to be.
