# Jurisprudence I — case illustrations and limits
**Created:** 2026-10-27 23:15 IST  
**Purpose:** Carefully selected legal examples for law, justice, validity, interpretation, dignity and social consequences. These cases supplement the theory syllabus; the seven syllabus topics are still primarily about jurisprudential thinkers and ideas.

## How to cite a case in K-1001
Use the sequence **facts → issue → holding → ratio/reasoning → relevance to the theory → limitation**. A case is not proof that a judge adopted a philosopher’s theory unless the judgment actually supports that attribution. The citations below are familiar law-report references; consult the full judgment/report before memorising detailed passages.

## 1. Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala
**Citation:** (1973) 4 SCC 225  
**Court:** Supreme Court of India; 13-judge bench; decision in 1973.  
**Background/facts:** The litigation arose from challenges involving Kerala land-reform measures and constitutional amendments. The central constitutional issue concerned the extent of Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.  
**Issue:** Is Parliament’s amending power unlimited, or are there constitutional limits to the changes it can make?  
**Holding / principle:** The Court’s fractured majority established the basic-structure limitation: constitutional amendment power cannot be used to destroy the Constitution’s basic structure. The judges did not agree on a single exhaustive list of every basic feature.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** It illustrates that an institution’s formally conferred power may be subject to higher-order legal limits, and that constitutional identity and legal validity are not reducible to the bare will of a legislature. It can open a discussion of constitutionalism, legal hierarchy and the relationship between power and legal principle.  
**Caution:** Do not use it to claim that the Supreme Court adopted Kelsen’s Grundnorm or a particular natural-law theory. It is a constitutional doctrine with its own reasoning and history.  
**Primary-source research starting point:** A later Supreme Court judgment tracing the doctrine: https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2019/1827/1827_2019_1_1501_39619_Judgement_07-Nov-2022.pdf

## 2. Maneka Gandhi v Union of India
**Citation:** (1978) 1 SCC 248  
**Court/year:** Supreme Court of India, 1978.  
**Background/facts:** Maneka Gandhi challenged the impounding of her passport and the associated procedure.  
**Issue:** How should the constitutional guarantee of life and personal liberty, especially the “procedure established by law” requirement in Article 21, be understood?  
**Holding / principle:** The Court read Article 21’s procedural protection as requiring a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable, and read fundamental-rights guarantees in a connected rather than wholly isolated way.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** A useful illustration of interpretation: the meaning of a legal text develops through judicial reasoning, precedent and constitutional principles. It can support a discussion of formal validity versus substantive fairness and of rules whose application requires interpretation.  
**Caution:** State the actual constitutional holding; do not label the decision “a Hart case” or “a realist case” without explaining and supporting the analytical connection.

## 3. Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India
**Citation:** (2017) 10 SCC 1  
**Court/year:** Supreme Court of India, nine-judge bench, 24 August 2017.  
**Background/facts:** The Court considered whether privacy enjoys constitutional protection, in the course of litigation concerning Aadhaar and related questions.  
**Issue:** Is privacy a constitutionally protected right, and what is its relationship with liberty, dignity and autonomy?  
**Holding / principle:** The nine-judge bench unanimously recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right, grounded in liberty and dignity and linked to Part III of the Constitution.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** It illustrates how legal interpretation uses constitutional text, precedent, underlying values and conceptions of personhood. It is useful for a careful natural-law/rights discussion, but does not establish that the Court adopted a single natural-law philosophy.  
**Official Supreme Court judgment:** https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2012/35071/35071_2012_Judgement_24-Aug-2017.pdf

## 4. Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India
**Citation:** (2018) 10 SCC 1  
**Court/year:** Supreme Court of India, 2018.  
**Background/facts:** Petitioners challenged the application of section 377 of the former Indian Penal Code to consensual sexual conduct between adults of the same sex.  
**Issue:** Whether criminalizing consensual adult same-sex intimacy was compatible with constitutional guarantees, including equality, liberty and dignity.  
**Holding / principle:** The Court read down section 377 IPC insofar as it criminalized consensual sexual conduct between adults of the same sex; the decision did not immunize non-consensual acts or conduct involving animals. The opinions discuss dignity, autonomy, equality and constitutional morality.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** The case is useful for explaining how constitutional principles may constrain a rule with a history of moral disapproval, and for discussing the relationship between social morality and constitutional morality.  
**Critical current-law note:** This is a historical case about section 377 IPC. Do not copy the old IPC section number into a current BNS answer or assume that a former section maps one-to-one into the BNS. If current criminal liability is the question, use the statutory text applicable to the relevant date and conduct.  
**Official judgment:** https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2016/14961/14961_2016_Judgement_06-Sep-2018.pdf

## 5. Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation
**Citation:** (1985) 3 SCC 545; also reported as 1986 AIR 180.  
**Court/year:** Supreme Court of India, judgment dated 10 July 1985.  
**Background/facts:** Pavement and slum residents challenged threatened eviction/removal, arguing that removal threatened their means of livelihood and implicated constitutional protections.  
**Issue:** Whether livelihood forms part of the protection of life under Article 21, and how that relates to municipal authority over public places.  
**Holding / principle:** The Court recognized the link between livelihood and the right to life, while not granting an unrestricted right to occupy public pavements. The case addresses procedure and the relationship between livelihood and regulation of public space.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** A strong illustration for Pound’s question about balancing competing interests and for the sociological school’s attention to the practical circumstances of people affected by law. It also illustrates why a legal right and the limits placed on it must both be stated.  
**Official judgment text:** https://api.sci.gov.in/jonew/judis/9246.pdf

## 6. R v Dudley and Stephens
**Citation:** (1884) 14 QBD 273.  
**Court/year:** Queen’s Bench Division, England, 1884.  
**Background/facts:** After a shipwreck, sailors in an open boat killed and ate a young cabin boy in an attempt to survive; they argued that necessity justified their conduct.  
**Issue:** Could necessity excuse the intentional killing in these circumstances?  
**Holding / principle:** The court rejected necessity as a defence to murder on the facts.  
**Jurisprudential relevance:** A classic illustration of the tension among moral necessity, survival, legal rules and the limits of judicial recognition of excuses. It can be used in a law/morality question to show that a court may reject a moral argument as a legal defence.  
**Caution:** It is an English historical case, not a statement of the current Indian criminal code. If a question asks about current Indian law, analyze the applicable Indian statute and precedent.

## 7. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India
**Citation:** (1984) 3 SCC 161.  
**Court/year:** Supreme Court of India, 1984.  
**Background/facts:** A public-interest petition highlighted bonded labourers working in stone quarries near Faridabad and their harsh living and working conditions.  
**Issue:** How should constitutional and statutory protections be enforced for vulnerable workers who may face severe barriers to ordinary legal processes?  
**Principle/relevance:** The Court addressed the State’s obligations toward bonded labourers and the practical barriers poverty and social disadvantage create in accessing justice. This can illustrate sociological jurisprudence’s attention to law in operation and material conditions.  
**Caution:** The case belongs to its constitutional and statutory setting. Do not attribute its holding directly to Pound or Duguit.

## 8. Case use in the answer: worked example
**Question:** “Explain the relationship between law and morality.”  
A strong answer defines legal validity and moral justification separately, outlines natural-law and positivist positions, then gives *Navtej Singh Johar* as a constitutional example of a court evaluating criminalization by reference to equality, dignity and autonomy. It explains what the case held, notes that “social morality” and “constitutional morality” are not identical, and avoids claiming that all immoral laws are legally invalid in every legal theory.

## 9. Statutory-section/version protocol for jurisprudence
K-1001 is a theory paper; most of its main topics do not have a specific controlling “current section”. Where a case involves a provision that later changed—such as *Navtej*’s former IPC section 377—identify the provision as it existed at the time, and do not silently carry it into the new criminal-law regime. For a topic that depends on current statutes, check the official consolidated text and commencement/application provisions.
