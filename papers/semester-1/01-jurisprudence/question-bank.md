## 2. Very short answers — 4/5 marks
Answer in a compact paragraph or a small set of well-developed points, according to the paper’s instructions.

### Q1. What is the Grundnorm?
In Kelsen’s Pure Theory, the Grundnorm is the presupposed basic norm that serves as the ultimate point of validity for interpreting a legal order as a unified normative system. A lower norm is valid because it is created under a higher authorizing norm. The Grundnorm is not an ordinary enacted statute or a literal section of the Constitution. Its role is to explain the normative unity of the legal order.

### Q2. Explain Volksgeist.
Volksgeist is Savigny’s idea of the shared historical legal consciousness of a people. Law develops organically through custom, communal practices and legal tradition rather than being created entirely by a legislator at one moment. Jurists later help articulate and systematize the law. The approach explains historical development but can be criticised for assuming a unified social spirit and for romanticizing custom.

### Q3. What is “natural law with variable content”?
The phrase is associated with Rudolf Stammler. It distinguishes a rational ideal of just legal/social ordering from the particular rules that must change as society’s circumstances change. The theory tries to preserve a standard for evaluating positive law without requiring the same substantive rules for every time and place. It is criticised for being abstract and difficult to apply in hard cases.

### Q4. Explain social solidarity.
For Léon Duguit, human beings are interdependent and social life depends on cooperation and the performance of social functions. Law is related to objective obligations arising from this interdependence, rather than being explained only as the command of an unlimited sovereign. Public power must be exercised in accordance with social functions. A limitation is that “solidarity” may not specify a single answer when social interests conflict.

### Q5. What is social engineering?
Roscoe Pound’s social-engineering theory treats law as a means of ordering social relations by identifying and reconciling competing individual, public and social interests, with a view to reducing friction and avoidable waste. It asks how rules work in practice as well as what they state. Criticism: balancing interests involves value choices and can conceal unequal power.

### Q6. State Maine’s status-to-contract proposition.
Maine argued that the movement of progressive societies has broadly been from status to contract: rights and duties increasingly depend on voluntary legal relations rather than being fixed entirely by inherited/social position. It is a historical thesis, not a universal law. Modern societies retain status-based rules and regulate contractual relations.

### Q7. Distinguish rule skepticism and fact skepticism.
Rule skepticism questions whether abstract legal rules always determine a single inevitable result. Fact skepticism, associated particularly with Jerome Frank, emphasizes uncertainty in the facts as found by trial courts due to evidence, credibility and fact-finding. Neither means that judges can act arbitrarily or that legal rules are irrelevant.

### Q8. What does Austin mean by command?
Austin’s model identifies a command as an expression of a superior’s wish that another person act or refrain from acting, backed by a threatened sanction. The person addressed has a duty, and the theory places the command within a structure of political sovereignty. Its limitations include difficulty explaining power-conferring rules, custom and legal limits on authority.

### Q9. Explain Hart’s rule of recognition.
The rule of recognition provides criteria used by officials to identify valid legal norms within a legal system. It is a social rule accepted and used from an internal point of view by officials; it is not simply the personal command of a sovereign. It is part of Hart’s account of secondary rules, alongside rules of change and adjudication.

### Q10. What is the Marxist economic approach to law?
The Marxist approach studies law in relation to material production, property, labour, class and economic power. It asks whether formally neutral rules stabilize existing social/economic relationships and whether legal equality coexists with unequal material resources. A careful account avoids economic determinism: law can also mediate conflict and support reform.

### Q11. Explain “law in action”.
The phrase captures the need to study how legal rules and institutions actually function, not only how legal doctrines are written. It is central to sociological jurisprudence and legal realism, though the schools differ: Pound examines interest reconciliation; American realists emphasize actual decision-making, facts and institutional behaviour.

### Q12. Differentiate expository and censorial jurisprudence.
The terms are associated with Bentham. Expository jurisprudence describes what the law is; censorial jurisprudence evaluates what the law ought to be, commonly by reference to a reform standard such as utility. This distinction highlights the difference between describing positive law and criticizing it.

## 3. Ten-mark questions — answer plans
For each, write a direct definition, 4–6 organized headings, one comparison/criticism and a conclusion.

1. **Revival of natural law:** explain Stammler’s variable content; Kohler’s law/culture connection; compare; assess abstraction and cultural relativism.
2. **Austin’s command theory:** command, duty, sanction, sovereign, positive law; explain its clarity; critique constitutional limits, custom, international law and power-conferring rules.
3. **Hart’s theory:** primary/secondary rules, recognition/change/adjudication, internal point of view, open texture; compare with Austin.
4. **Savigny’s Volksgeist:** historical background; custom and organic growth; jurists; codification debate; limits of tradition.
5. **Maine’s status-to-contract:** define status and contract, historical method, examples, and criticisms of linear evolution.
6. **Duguit’s social solidarity:** interdependence, objective law, public function, critique of sovereign-will theory, benefits and limits.
7. **Pound’s social engineering:** explain interest categories and method, example, merits and criticism.
8. **American realism:** Holmes, Llewellyn, Frank; law in action; rule/fact skepticism; limits.
9. **Marxist economic approach:** historical materialism, base/superstructure, class/property, strengths, objections to determinism.
10. **Natural law vs positivism:** compare legal validity, morality, injustice, certainty and criticism; avoid treating either school as completely uniform.

## 4. Twenty-mark essay questions — high-priority
1. Define jurisprudence. Discuss its nature, scope and importance.
2. “Jurisprudence is the formal science of positive law.” Discuss in light of different definitions.
3. Trace the development of natural law and critically explain the revival through Stammler and Kohler.
4. Explain Austin’s command theory and critically evaluate its adequacy for a modern legal system.
5. Explain Kelsen’s Pure Theory, hierarchy of norms and Grundnorm.
6. Discuss Hart’s theory of primary and secondary rules. How does it improve on Austin’s account?
7. Compare Austin, Kelsen and Hart.
8. Explain Savigny’s historical school and the concept of Volksgeist. What is the position on codification?
9. Discuss Maine’s status-to-contract proposition and its limitations as a general account of legal development.
10. Critically examine Pound’s social-engineering theory, including types of interests and reconciliation.
11. Explain Duguit’s concept of social solidarity and its implications for public authority.
12. “American modern realism combines analytical and sociological insights.” Discuss carefully.
13. Explain the Marxist economic approach to law and critically assess the base/superstructure account.
14. Distinguish law as it is from law as it ought to be. Discuss with reference to positivism and natural law.
15. Compare the historical, sociological and Marxist approaches to legal development.

## 5. Comparison-question checklist
If asked to compare, always include at least these five axes:
- central thesis and the thinker’s key phrase;
- what counts as law / where legal authority comes from;
- relationship between validity and morality;
- explanation of legal change and social effect;
- principal strength and criticism.

## 6. Recent/modern application questions
These are extension questions, not a promise of examination appearance:
1. How does constitutional interpretation illustrate the distinction between text, validity and moral justification? You may use *Maneka Gandhi* or *Puttaswamy* with exact holdings.
2. Can a legal rule be valid but unjust? Compare a positivist answer with a natural-law answer.
3. Does the existence of formal equality establish substantive equality? Evaluate through a Marxist lens, while separating analysis from current legal doctrine.
4. Does social purpose justify every restriction on individual rights? Compare Pound’s balancing approach with constitutional limitations.
5. Does judicial uncertainty mean judicial arbitrariness? Explain from the realist perspective.
