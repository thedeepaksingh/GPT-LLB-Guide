# Topic 2 — Natural Law School
**Paper:** K-1001 · **Created:** 2026-10-27 23:12 IST  
**Syllabus anchor:** Classical natural law; revival of natural law—Rudolf Stammler and Kohler.  
**Status:** Study draft; statutory section numbers are not central to this theory.

## 1. Core proposition
Natural-law theories maintain, in differing ways, that law is connected to reason, justice, human nature, moral order or standards not exhausted by a sovereign’s command or a text’s enactment. The school is not one single doctrine. Its thinkers disagree about the source of moral standards, whether unjust rules are “not law” or legally valid but defective, and how universal principles apply to changing social conditions.

**Exam warning:** Avoid the oversimplification “natural law means every unjust law is automatically void.” Some natural-law thinkers use that language strongly; others distinguish legal existence from moral legitimacy.

## 2. Historical background
Natural-law thinking developed from Greek philosophical inquiry into justice and nature, through Roman and Christian thought, Enlightenment theories of rights, and modern revival. It has influenced arguments about human dignity, limits on political power, rights and the moral justification of legal systems. It has also been criticised because abstract moral standards may be disputed, and historical claims about “natural” social order have sometimes supported hierarchy as well as freedom.

## 3. Classical approaches
### A. Greek thought
- **Socrates:** associated with questioning, reasoned moral inquiry and the relation between obedience, justice and law; avoid attributing a fully developed modern natural-law system to him.
- **Plato:** connected justice with an ordered moral/political community and the pursuit of the good.
- **Aristotle:** distinguished forms of justice and discussed natural justice as having a claim beyond merely local enactment, while recognizing that some legal rules depend on convention. He is not simply a modern rights theorist.
- **Stoics:** emphasized universal reason and a rational order shared by human beings; this supported ideas of a moral law transcending local political boundaries.

### B. Cicero and Roman thought
Cicero articulated an influential conception of true law as right reason consistent with nature and of broader application than the commands of one city. The central importance is the claim that standards of justice need not be reducible to local enactment.

### C. Thomas Aquinas
Aquinas’s account is often taught through four forms of law:
1. **Eternal law:** the rational ordering of the universe in divine reason.
2. **Natural law:** rational creatures’ participation in eternal law, grasped through reason.
3. **Human law:** rules made by human authorities for the common good; they should derive from reason and natural law.
4. **Divine law:** revealed law, guiding human beings toward an ultimate end.

Human law has a role in specifying general principles for social life; not every positive rule is directly deduced from a single universal command. Aquinas’s theory links law to reason, public good and legitimate authority. His account of an unjust law is complex: unjust enactments may fail as moral obligations in important circumstances, though consequences and the need to avoid disorder matter.

### D. Early modern natural law and social contract
- **Hugo Grotius:** helped develop natural-law reasoning in a form that was not wholly dependent on a specific theological premise; reason and principles of social life play central roles.
- **Thomas Hobbes:** used a state-of-nature account and laws of nature concerned with peace and self-preservation; his conclusions differ sharply from Locke’s.
- **John Locke:** argued that natural rights, including life, liberty and property, constrain political authority and support a right to resist certain abuses.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** explored freedom, legitimate political authority and the general will. He should not be treated as simply interchangeable with Locke.
- **Immanuel Kant:** grounded legal and moral reasoning in freedom and rational autonomy; a distinct philosophical approach rather than merely a natural-rights checklist.

These thinkers are often included in broader histories of natural-law thought, but K-1001 specifically names classical natural law and the revival through Stammler and Kohler. Prioritize those named syllabus areas first.

## 4. Revival of natural law
The “revival” does not mean a return unchanged to medieval theology. Modern theorists sought to retain a critical standard for law while recognizing historical, cultural and social change.

### A. Rudolf Stammler — natural law with variable content
**Central thesis:** Stammler sought a formal, rational ideal for evaluating positive law while accepting that the substantive content of law must respond to changing social conditions. He is often associated with “natural law with variable content.”

Key points:
- The legal ideal is not a fixed list of substantive rules valid in every society and age.
- Law is evaluated by reference to a just or right form of social ordering, with the content of particular rules adapting to social life.
- His distinction between form and changing content attempts to avoid both rigid traditional natural law and uncritical legal positivism.
- Justice is not reducible to whatever happens to be enacted; positive law remains open to critical evaluation.

**Illustration:** A rule about work, property or family may require different concrete content as social and economic conditions change, while its justification must still be assessed against a rational ideal of fair social ordering.

**Criticism:** The ideal standards can seem abstract; different observers may disagree about what the just form requires; and the formal ideal may not yield a clear answer to every concrete dispute.

### B. Rudolf von Jhering — context only
Jhering is often studied as a bridge from formal jurisprudence toward social-purpose analysis. His emphasis on law’s purposes and interests helps explain the broader move away from treating law as a self-contained logical system. However, the CCSU K-1001 outline specifically names Stammler and Kohler in the natural-law revival and Pound/Duguit in sociological jurisprudence; use Jhering as context, not as a replacement for the prescribed thinkers.

### C. Josef Kohler
Kohler linked law with civilization and cultural development. Legal principles and institutions must be understood in the context of a society’s cultural life and changing civilizational needs. Law is not an abstract, timeless code detached from social development; it is part of a culture’s efforts to order communal life.

Key points:
- Legal forms need to be understood in relation to the culture and social stage in which they operate.
- Law has a role in promoting and organizing civilized social life.
- Legal development is historically and culturally situated, but should still be assessed by its social and civilizational function.

**Criticism:** “Civilization” is a contested and potentially ethnocentric standard; cultural context alone does not establish that a rule is just; and the theory needs criteria to protect individuals when a dominant culture treats them unfairly.

**Important distinction:** Stammler emphasizes a formal ideal of just law with variable social content; Kohler emphasizes the law-culture relationship and legal development in civilization. Do not collapse their positions.

## 5. Main features often associated with natural-law theories
- a standard of evaluation beyond mere enactment or command;
- a role for reason and moral judgment;
- claims of generality or universality, although their precise content is disputed;
- a way to criticize legally recognized power;
- a connection between law, justice and legitimate authority;
- different accounts of the source of the standard: reason, nature, divine order, autonomy, rights or social rationality.

## 6. Strengths and criticisms
**Strengths**
1. Provides a principled basis for criticising oppressive or arbitrary rules.
2. Helps explain the moral vocabulary of human dignity, rights and limits on state power.
3. Encourages the claim that legality alone does not settle legitimacy.
4. Supports evaluation of legal reform by reasoned standards rather than power alone.

**Criticisms**
1. Natural-law theories disagree about the content and source of the supposed universal standard.
2. Universal language can mask the values of a particular society or group.
3. If morality and validity are not carefully distinguished, the theory may make it difficult to describe an unjust rule that officials still apply.
4. Moral appeals may be indeterminate without institutional methods for resolving disputes.
5. A fixed conception of “human nature” or “civilization” can be used to justify exclusion.

## 7. Natural law and positive law — comparison
| Point | Natural-law approach | Positivist approach |
|---|---|---|
| Central inquiry | Relationship among law, reason, justice and moral standards | Social sources and criteria of legal validity |
| Validity/justice | Moral considerations have a fundamental role in evaluating law; views vary on legal validity | Legal validity is generally distinguished from moral merit |
| Unjust rule | May be condemned as defective in a deep legal/moral sense | Can be valid law yet morally objectionable |
| Change | May call for reform based on rational or moral standards | Explains change through recognized legal sources and procedures |
| Risk | Disagreement over moral standards | Risk of under-explaining legitimacy if validity is treated as the only issue |

This table presents broad tendencies, not a claim that all thinkers in either tradition share one doctrine.

## 8. Indian constitutional relevance
Indian constitutional adjudication often invokes dignity, liberty, equality, constitutional morality and limits on public power. For instance:
- *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225, is central to the constitutional amendment/basic-structure doctrine.
- *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India*, (1978) 1 SCC 248, interpreted Article 21’s procedure requirement as fair, just and reasonable.
- *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India*, (2017) 10 SCC 1, recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right.
- *Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India*, (2018) 10 SCC 1, stressed dignity, autonomy and constitutional morality.

These cases can illustrate legal reasoning informed by values. Do not say that they conclusively adopted classical natural law or Stammler’s theory; their holdings must be explained in their constitutional context.

## 9. Model 20-mark question and answer
### Q. What is natural law? Trace its historical development and explain the revival of natural law with special reference to Stammler and Kohler.
**Introduction.** Natural law is a family of theories asserting that positive law is related to reason, justice, human nature or moral standards that cannot be reduced to enactment alone. Its thinkers differ on the source and precise content of those standards.

**Classical development.** Greek philosophy raised questions about justice beyond local rules. Aristotle distinguished natural justice from justice dependent on convention, while Stoic thought emphasized universal reason. Cicero developed the idea of right reason consistent with nature. Aquinas later integrated natural law into a broader account of eternal, natural, human and divine law, linking human law with reason and the common good. Early modern thinkers including Grotius, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Kant offered different accounts of reason, freedom, rights and political authority.

**Revival.** Modern revival attempted to retain a critical ideal of law without simply reviving a fixed medieval code. Stammler’s “natural law with variable content” distinguishes a rational ideal or form of just ordering from the changing substantive rules required by different social conditions. Positive law must be adapted to life, yet remains open to evaluation.

Kohler emphasized law’s relationship with culture and civilization. Law must be understood through the social and cultural development of the community and its role in ordering civilized social life. His approach is more historically and culturally situated than a rigid list of universal rules.

**Comparison.** Stammler principally emphasizes a formal ideal of justice whose legal content varies; Kohler emphasizes the cultural context and civilizational function of legal development. Both resist a purely mechanical treatment of enacted rules, but they do so differently.

**Criticism.** Natural-law reasoning supplies a basis for criticizing oppressive law, but its standards may be disputed. Stammler’s formal ideal may be too abstract to resolve specific conflicts; Kohler’s appeal to civilization may be indeterminate or culturally biased. Moral criticism must also be distinguished from the institutional question of what a court is legally authorized to enforce.

**Conclusion.** Natural law remains influential because it insists that legal power deserves critical evaluation. Modern revivalists attempted to combine that evaluative ambition with recognition that law’s concrete content must respond to changing social life.

## 10. Short-note templates
**Stammler:** A modern revivalist who is commonly associated with “natural law with variable content”. He distinguished a rational ideal of just social ordering from the concrete legal rules that change with social conditions. This avoids a permanently fixed substantive code while retaining a standard for assessing positive law. His theory is criticised for abstraction and uncertainty about how the ideal resolves hard cases.

**Kohler:** Kohler understood law in relation to culture and civilizational development. Legal institutions should be interpreted in their social context and assessed by their role in organizing communal life. The approach highlights legal change but faces the objection that “civilization” is an uncertain standard and culture alone cannot establish justice.
