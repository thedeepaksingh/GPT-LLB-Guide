# K-1001 — Master comparison of jurisprudential schools
**Created:** 2026-10-27 23:15 IST · **Status:** Revision resource  
Use this after studying each topic. The marks in an answer usually come from explaining the theory, not only reproducing a table.

## 1. One-page map
| School / thinker | Core question | Central concept | What it explains well | Main risk/criticism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Natural law — classical | What does reason/nature/justice require of law? | Law and a higher moral/rational order | Moral evaluation and limits on arbitrary power | Disagreement over universal standards |
| Stammler | How can law be evaluated while content changes? | Natural law with variable content | A formal ideal responsive to social life | Abstract ideal may be hard to apply |
| Kohler | How does law relate to culture and civilization? | Law as culturally situated | Historical/cultural development | “Civilization” may be vague or biased |
| Bentham | How should law be described and reformed? | Utility; expository/censorial jurisprudence | Codification and rational reform | Aggregated welfare can threaten rights |
| Austin | What is positive law in command terms? | Sovereign, command, duty, sanction | Source, authority and coercion | Custom, constitutional limits and power-conferring rules |
| Kelsen | How are legal norms valid within an order? | Hierarchy of norms, Grundnorm | Normative structure and competence | Ultimate norm is abstract/hypothetical |
| Hart | What makes a modern legal system? | Primary/secondary rules; rule of recognition | Legal recognition, change and adjudication | Hard cases and principles test the account |
| Savigny | How does law grow historically? | Volksgeist; organic growth | Custom, legal history and jurists | Romanticizes a unified “people”; tradition may be unjust |
| Maine | How do legal institutions change? | Status to contract | Comparative historical transformation | Overly linear or culturally limited |
| Pound | How should law order conflicting social claims? | Social engineering and interests | Law’s function and practical consequences | Balancing may hide power/values |
| Duguit | What duties arise from interdependence? | Social solidarity and public function | Limits on public power and social obligations | Solidarity may be indeterminate |
| American realism | How do courts actually decide? | Law in action; prediction; rule/fact skepticism | Institutional behaviour and uncertainty | Prediction alone does not justify a decision |
| Marxist approach | How do economic relations shape law? | Base/superstructure, class, property and labour | Structural power and material inequality | Crude economic determinism |

## 2. Compare the schools by one common set of questions
| Question | Natural law | Analytical positivism (broad) | Historical | Sociological | American realism | Marxist/economic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source/validity | Moral/rational standard matters; models differ | Recognized social/legal sources and validity criteria | Historical practices/institutions shape law | Rules and institutions operate in social settings | Institutional practices and likely outcomes | Material/economic relations influence legal structures |
| Law and morality | Connected in a fundamental way | Validity distinguished from moral merit | Historical explanation does not itself justify | Social needs and consequences matter | Descriptive/predictive focus; not itself a moral test | Evaluates class/power and material effects |
| Legal change | Rational/moral critique or renewed standard | Rules of recognition/change, legislation, precedent | Gradual evolution, custom, institutional history | Response to social problems/interests | Decisions and practice reveal change in action | Changes in production/class relations and political struggle |
| Typical weakness | Moral standards contested | May understate legitimacy or social facts | Can romanticize history | Interests/solidarity may be hard to rank | May understate normative constraints | May overstate economic determination |

## 3. High-yield distinctions
### Austin vs Kelsen vs Hart
- **Austin:** law is modelled as sovereign command backed by sanction.
- **Kelsen:** legal validity is explained through a hierarchy of norms and an ultimate presupposed Grundnorm.
- **Hart:** a developed legal system combines duty-imposing primary rules with secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication.
Do not substitute one theory’s central term for another’s.

### Savigny vs Maine
- Savigny: law grows in a community’s historical legal consciousness.
- Maine: comparative historical development; the status-to-contract formula.
A question about codification points naturally to Savigny; a question quoting “status to contract” points to Maine.

### Pound vs Duguit
- Pound asks how competing interests can be reconciled.
- Duguit asks how social interdependence creates objective obligations and constrains public authority.

### Realism vs positivism
Realism is not simply another word for positivism. Positivist theories ask about the social sources and criteria of legal validity; realists focus on how legal institutions actually operate and how outcomes are influenced by facts and behaviour. They can overlap, but they ask different questions.

### Sociological vs Marxist approaches
Both relate law to society. Pound’s focus is interest reconciliation and social function; Marxist approaches focus more directly on material production, property, class and the distribution of power. Neither should be reduced to “law changes with society” without the theory’s distinctive mechanism.

## 4. How to deploy this in an exam
For a direct comparison, use the same axes for both thinkers: central thesis; source/validity; relationship with morality; account of change; strengths; criticisms. Finish with a reasoned conclusion. Do not merely copy this whole table into every essay—use only relevant rows and explain them.
