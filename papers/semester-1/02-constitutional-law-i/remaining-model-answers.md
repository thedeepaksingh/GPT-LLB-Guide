# K-1002 — Answer key for remaining question-bank prompts and problems

## Ten-mark answers

### Q1. Salient features of the Constitution of India
The Constitution is a detailed written framework for institutions, powers, rights and public responsibilities. Its salient features include popular sovereignty expressed in the Preamble; parliamentary democracy; a federal distribution of powers with a strong Union; Fundamental Rights; Directive Principles; an independent judiciary and judicial review; constitutional supremacy and rule of law; adult suffrage; single citizenship; emergency provisions; and a special amendment procedure.

These features operate together. Fundamental Rights constrain public power, while Directive Principles guide social and economic policy. Federalism distributes authority, while emergency and concurrent powers permit central action in defined circumstances. Article 368 allows amendment but, under *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), cannot be used to destroy the basic structure. *Minerva Mills* (1980) reinforces limited amending power and balance between Parts III and IV.

The Constitution is therefore neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible, and neither purely federal nor wholly unitary. Its distinctive character is a democratic constitutional order combining effective government with enforceable limits.

### Q2. Legal status of the Preamble
The Preamble states the Constitution's foundational objectives: justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, and identifies India as a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic. It expresses popular sovereignty and guides constitutional interpretation.

In *In re Berubari Union* (1960), the Court expressed an earlier view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) clarified that it is part of the Constitution and relevant to interpretation. The Preamble may illuminate constitutional values and basic-structure analysis, but it cannot override clear operative text or ordinarily provide a freestanding cause of action by itself. It can be amended under Article 368, subject to the basic-structure limit.

### Q3. Federalism with a strong Union
India has federal features: a written and supreme Constitution, distribution of legislative powers under Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule, elected State institutions, an independent judiciary and special amendment safeguards for specified federal provisions. Centralizing features include residuary power, Parliament's concurrent and exceptional State List powers, emergency provisions, Article 356 and single citizenship.

In *State of West Bengal v Union of India* (1963), the Court discussed the constitutional relationship between the Union and States. *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994) treated federalism as a basic feature and affirmed judicial review of Article 356 action. India is best described as a federation with a strong Union, not as a collection of sovereign States or a wholly unitary system. Central powers remain subject to constitutional conditions and judicial review.

### Q4. Basic structure: justification and limits
Article 368 provides the constitutional amendment procedure. In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure. The doctrine protects constitutional identity while allowing legal and political adaptation.

Features recognized in different judgments include constitutional supremacy, democracy, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review and limited amending power. There is no exhaustive list that automatically decides every case. *Minerva Mills* (1980) held that limited amending power is itself basic and emphasized the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. *I.R. Coelho* (2007) applied basic-structure review to certain Ninth Schedule insertions made after the relevant cut-off.

The doctrine is defended as a safeguard against a temporary parliamentary supermajority abolishing foundational commitments. Critics question the doctrine's textual precision and the risk of judicial subjectivity. It is nevertheless binding constitutional doctrine. It does not prohibit amendment; it prohibits destruction of the Constitution's basic structure.

### Q5. Article 12 and State instrumentality
Article 12 includes Union and State governments and legislatures, local authorities and “other authorities” within its wording. Courts interpret “other authorities” contextually to determine whether an entity is an instrumentality or agency of the State. *R.D. Shetty v International Airport Authority of India* (1979) and *Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib* (1981) identify factors such as government ownership and financial support, deep and pervasive control, public functions, monopoly status and transfer of governmental functions.

No single factor is invariably conclusive. Government shareholding or public importance alone does not automatically make every entity “State”; the institutional relationship and facts matter. Article 12 status can permit claims under provisions such as Articles 14 and 16, but the scope of the particular right must also be established.

### Q6. Article 14: classification and arbitrariness
Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws to any person within its scope. The traditional classification test requires an intelligible differentia and a rational relation between that distinction and the law's objective. Both limbs must be satisfied.

Article 14 doctrine also addresses arbitrary State action. *E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu* (1974) linked equality and arbitrariness in its context. The applicable test depends on the kind of State action and the relevant precedent; not every dispute is resolved by reciting only the two-part classification formula. A proper answer identifies the comparator, the classification, the purpose, the evidence supporting the distinction and the applicable constitutional standard.

### Q7. Article 19 freedoms and limitations
Article 19(1) protects specified freedoms of citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly without arms, association, movement, residence/settlement and profession/occupation/trade/business. The former property-related freedom is no longer included in Article 19.

Each freedom has its own constitutional limitation clause. Article 19(2), for example, governs restrictions on speech and lists its authorized grounds; those grounds cannot be freely imported into clauses governing other freedoms. A challenge should identify the claimant's citizenship, the precise freedom, the legal basis for the restriction, the exact permitted ground, and the applicable reasonableness or proportionality analysis. *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015) is a major speech case involving Section 66A of the Information Technology Act; its holding should not be generalized to every speech restriction.

### Q8. Article 21: doctrinal development
Article 21 provides that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. *A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras* (1950) adopted a more compartmentalized approach to Fundamental Rights. *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978) required a procedure affecting life or liberty to be fair, just and reasonable and read Articles 14, 19 and 21 in an interconnected manner where applicable. *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* (2017) recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right grounded in liberty and dignity.

Article 21 is broad but not unlimited. Identify the State action, the interest affected, the applicable procedure and constitutional test, and any lawful limitations. Current statutes and later judgments must be checked for the facts at issue.

### Q9. Article 20 protections
Article 20 contains three distinct protections: no retrospective criminal conviction or greater penalty in the terms of Article 20(1); protection against prosecution and punishment for the same offence under Article 20(2); and protection against compelled self-incrimination under Article 20(3). Each clause has its own elements. Do not equate the constitutional double-jeopardy clause with every statutory rule about successive proceedings. *State of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad* and *Selvi v State of Karnataka* are relevant to testimonial compulsion and forms of evidence; state their precise holdings only after checking the judgments.

### Q10. Article 22 and arrest/preventive detention
For ordinary arrest, Article 22 generally provides that the person be informed of grounds, be allowed to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, and be produced before a magistrate within the constitutionally prescribed period, subject to textual exceptions. Preventive detention is treated in separate clauses with different safeguards and exceptions. Do not apply every ordinary-arrest safeguard identically to preventive detention.

The constitutional text must be distinguished from the procedural statute in force. For contemporary facts, check the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and any applicable savings or transitional provisions. *D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* is an important authority on arrest and custodial safeguards, read with current law.

### Q11. Articles 25–28: freedom of religion
Article 25 protects freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other provisions of Part III, and permits specified regulation of secular activity and social reform. Article 26 protects specified rights of religious denominations, subject to constitutional limits. Article 27 addresses taxes specifically appropriated for promoting or maintaining a particular religion. Article 28 regulates religious instruction or worship in specified educational institutions.

The analysis must distinguish belief, religious practice, denominational autonomy, secular administration and public regulation. *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar* (Shirur Mutt, 1954) is foundational; *Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977) considered propagation. The essential-religious-practices doctrine is contested and must not be reduced to an automatic test.

### Q12. Articles 29–30: cultural and educational rights
Article 29(1) protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it. Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on specified grounds only. Article 30 protects religious and linguistic minorities' right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

The provisions are related but distinct: Article 29(1) is framed for any qualifying section of citizens, while Article 30 specifically protects minority educational institutions. Regulation may be permitted for standards and proper administration, but cannot destroy the protected right. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka* (2002) is a leading authority; detailed propositions should be checked against the judgment.

### Q13. Articles 32 and 226
Article 32 guarantees access to the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”, giving it broader textual subject-matter scope. Both remain subject to jurisdictional rules and remedial doctrine.

Habeas corpus tests legality of detention; mandamus directs performance of a public/legal duty; prohibition restrains a lower court or tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction before completion; certiorari quashes decisions for recognized legal defects; quo warranto tests entitlement to public office. Select the writ based on the right, respondent, duty, jurisdiction and stage of proceedings.

### Q14. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
Fundamental Rights in Part III are enforceable according to their terms and limitations. Directive Principles in Part IV guide the State toward social and economic welfare; Article 37 says they are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in governance. In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), a Directive Principle could not override an inconsistent Fundamental Right under the constitutional position then applicable. *Minerva Mills* (1980) emphasized harmony and balance between Parts III and IV. The two Parts have different roles but must be read within the whole constitutional scheme.

### Q15. Judicial review and constitutionalism
Judicial review enables courts to examine whether laws and public action comply with the Constitution. Articles 13, 32 and 226 support review of ordinary laws and enforcement of Fundamental Rights; review of constitutional amendments under Article 368 involves the distinct basic-structure doctrine. Judicial review protects constitutional supremacy, rights and federal limits, but courts must remain within jurisdiction, apply precedent and provide legal reasons. It is not a general power to substitute judicial policy preferences for every legislative or executive choice.

## Worked problem answers

### Problem A. Speech banned as “offensive” without a stated legal basis
**Issue:** Whether the ban violates Article 19(1)(a).  
**Rule:** Article 19(2) permits restrictions only on its enumerated grounds and subject to constitutional requirements. Offensiveness by itself is not a freestanding Article 19(2) ground.  
**Application:** The authority has not identified a statutory basis or a permitted ground. On the facts given, the ban appears constitutionally vulnerable. The State would need to identify valid legal authority, evidence connecting the restriction to an authorized ground, and compliance with the applicable reasonableness/proportionality standard.  
**Conclusion:** The ban is likely invalid on the stated facts, subject to any additional lawful basis and evidence.

### Problem B. Government-controlled company arbitrarily denies a benefit
**Issue:** Whether the entity is “State” under Article 12 and whether the denial violates Article 14.  
**Rule:** Apply the contextual instrumentality/agency factors in *R.D. Shetty* and *Ajay Hasia*; no single factor automatically decides the issue. If Article 12 applies, State action must satisfy Article 14.  
**Application:** Establish ownership, funding, depth of governmental control, public function, monopoly and whether governmental functions were transferred. If the entity qualifies and the benefit was denied arbitrarily or through unequal criteria, Article 14 may be engaged. More facts about the entity and decision process are needed.  
**Conclusion:** A claim may succeed if Article 12 status and arbitrary State action are established; government connection alone is insufficient.

### Problem C. Passport/liberty decision made without fair procedure
**Issue:** Whether the decision violates Article 21 and potentially Article 14.  
**Rule:** *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* requires procedure affecting life or personal liberty to be fair, just and reasonable; Articles 14, 19 and 21 may be read together where applicable. Statutory requirements and hearing obligations depend on the governing law and facts.  
**Application:** Identify the statutory power, reasons, notice/hearing requirements, urgency or exceptions, and whether the person had an effective opportunity to challenge the decision. A bare assertion of procedure is not enough if the procedure is arbitrary.  
**Conclusion:** The decision may be challenged if the procedure or exercise of power fails constitutional and statutory standards; remedy depends on the record and applicable law.

### Problem D. Arrested person not produced before a magistrate within the prescribed period
**Issue:** Whether Article 22's ordinary-arrest safeguard has been breached.  
**Rule:** Article 22 contains a constitutional requirement for production before the nearest magistrate within the prescribed period, subject to constitutional text and exceptions. Preventive detention is a separate category. Current procedural legislation must also be checked.  
**Application:** Establish the time of arrest, custody, any excluded journey time under the applicable rule, legal basis for detention and whether an exception applies. If no lawful exception explains the delay, detention is constitutionally vulnerable.  
**Conclusion:** On the assumed facts, the failure is likely unlawful and may support prompt judicial relief; the exact remedy depends on the record and current procedure.

### Problem E. Law regulates a claimed religious practice
**Issue:** Whether the measure infringes Article 25 or Article 26, and whether a constitutional limitation applies.  
**Rule:** Article 25 is subject to public order, morality, health and other Part III provisions and includes specified regulatory/social-reform powers. Article 26 protects denominational rights subject to constitutional limits.  
**Application:** Identify the precise practice and evidence, whether the claim concerns belief or conduct, whether it is a denominational matter, whether the regulated activity is secular, and which express limitation or other right applies. Consider relevant precedent carefully; “essential religious practice” analysis is contested and context-sensitive.  
**Conclusion:** Neither religious invocation nor State regulation is automatically decisive. Validity turns on the exact right, constitutional text, evidence, purpose and applicable precedent.
