## Q1. Explain the salient features of the Constitution of India. (Long answer)

### Introduction
The Constitution of India is the supreme legal framework that establishes public institutions, distributes governmental powers, protects rights and sets the principles by which public authority must operate. Its distinctive character lies in combining democratic government with enforceable constitutional limits.

### 1. Written and detailed Constitution
The Constitution sets out the principal institutions, powers, rights and procedures in a comprehensive written text. Its detail reflects India's size, diversity, historical circumstances and need to regulate Union–State relations and social transformation.

### 2. Popular sovereignty and the Preamble
The Preamble begins with “We, the People of India”, locating constitutional authority in the people. It declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and identifies justice, liberty, equality and fraternity as foundational objectives. The words “socialist”, “secular” and “integrity” were added by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976.

### 3. Parliamentary democracy
The Union follows a parliamentary system. The President is the constitutional head, while the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister exercises executive functions under the Constitution and is collectively responsible to the House of the People. The system links executive authority to legislative accountability.

### 4. Federal system with a strong Union
Legislative powers are distributed between the Union and States, notably through the Seventh Schedule. The Constitution also gives the Union significant powers and provides emergency mechanisms. India is therefore commonly described as federal with distinctive centralizing features. In *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994), the Supreme Court discussed federalism and secularism in reviewing the use of Article 356.

### 5. Fundamental Rights
Part III (Articles 12–35) protects specified rights and provides constitutional standards against which State action may be tested. Rights are not all absolute; their scope, restrictions and remedies depend on the text and judicial interpretation.

### 6. Directive Principles of State Policy
Part IV guides the State toward social and economic justice. Directive Principles are not enforceable in the same way as Fundamental Rights, but they are constitutionally important. In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India* (1980), the Court emphasized constitutional balance between Parts III and IV and the limited nature of the amending power.

### 7. Independent judiciary and judicial review
An independent judiciary adjudicates disputes and reviews laws and executive action for constitutional compliance. Judicial review protects constitutional supremacy, while courts must still act within their judicial role and jurisdiction.

### 8. Supremacy of the Constitution and rule of law
Public institutions possess only the powers given or permitted by law. Government action cannot be justified solely by political preference if it violates constitutional requirements. Rule of law, equality and accountable power are central to constitutionalism.

### 9. Amendment procedure subject to basic structure
Article 368 establishes a special amendment procedure. Some amendments require special parliamentary majorities; specified categories also require ratification by at least half of State legislatures. In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament cannot use its amendment power to destroy the Constitution's basic structure.

### 10. Secular and republican character
The State has no constitutionally established national religion and must operate consistently with constitutional religious freedom and equality. A republic has a non-hereditary head of State. The precise constitutional implications of secularism are developed through the text and case law, including *S.R. Bommai*.

### 11. Universal adult suffrage and constitutional bodies
The constitutional framework supports representative government through adult suffrage. Independent or constitutionally protected institutions—including the Election Commission and Comptroller and Auditor General—support electoral integrity and public accountability.

### 12. Emergency provisions and single citizenship
The Constitution provides emergency mechanisms under defined conditions, while their use remains subject to constitutional limits. It also provides for single Indian citizenship rather than separate citizenship of each State.

### Conclusion
The Constitution combines democratic legitimacy, federal distribution, rights, judicial review, social-policy commitments and a structured amendment process. These features must be read together: constitutional democracy requires both effective government and enforceable limits on public power.

**Authorities to mention:** Constitution of India, Preamble, Parts III and IV, Articles 14, 32, 226 and 368; *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973); *Minerva Mills* (1980); *S.R. Bommai* (1994).

## Q2. Discuss the significance and legal status of the Preamble. (Medium/long answer)

### Meaning and text
The Preamble is the introductory statement of constitutional purpose. It records that the Constitution is adopted by the people of India and describes India as a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic. It sets out the objectives of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, including individual dignity and national unity and integrity.

### Legal significance
1. **Expresses constitutional philosophy:** It identifies the values that inform the constitutional order.
2. **Aids interpretation:** Where operative provisions reasonably permit more than one interpretation, the Preamble can help identify the Constitution's purposes. It cannot override clear constitutional text.
3. **Part of the Constitution:** In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court recognized the Preamble as part of the Constitution and treated it as relevant to constitutional interpretation.
4. **Basic-structure relevance:** The Preamble's values help illuminate features that may form part of the Constitution's basic structure. The precise basic-structure analysis comes from the doctrine and case law, not from treating every Preamble word as a separate enforceable right.
5. **Amendability with limits:** The Preamble may be amended through Article 368, but an amendment cannot destroy the basic structure.
6. **Not ordinarily a freestanding cause of action:** A claimant generally must connect a grievance to an operative constitutional provision, legal power or enforceable right. The Preamble does not independently confer every specific remedy.

### Development in case law
In *In re Berubari Union* (1960), the Court expressed an earlier view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. This position was clarified by the larger-bench decision in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), which held that it is part of the Constitution and can aid interpretation.

### Conclusion
The Preamble is neither legally irrelevant nor a substitute for operative Articles. It is a constitutional compass: it explains the Constitution's purposes and assists interpretation, while enforceable claims must be grounded in the constitutional text and applicable doctrine.

## Q3. Explain constitutionalism and the supremacy of the Constitution. (Medium answer)

### Meaning of constitutionalism
Constitutionalism is the principle that public power must be limited, legally justified and accountable. It requires more than the existence of a document called a constitution. A government might possess a written constitution yet lack meaningful constitutionalism if public authorities exercise unchecked power.

### Main elements
- **Limited government:** Every public authority must act within its lawful powers.
- **Rule of law:** Power is exercised under public, knowable legal standards rather than arbitrary will.
- **Fundamental Rights:** Part III protects specified individual and group interests against unconstitutional State action.
- **Separation of functions and checks:** Legislature, executive and judiciary have distinct roles and mechanisms of accountability, though India does not adopt an absolute separation.
- **Judicial review:** Courts can examine the constitutionality of laws and public action.
- **Accountability and democracy:** Elections, legislative responsibility and transparent legal procedures help control public power.
- **Independent adjudication:** Courts must be able to decide disputes without improper interference.

### Supremacy of the Constitution in India
The Constitution is the highest legal framework for State institutions. Ordinary laws and executive actions must conform to it. Articles 13, 32 and 226 support constitutional review and remedies. Constitutional amendments follow Article 368 and are subject to the basic-structure limitation recognized in *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973).

### Important distinction
Parliament is a powerful democratic legislature, but its authority is constitutionally structured rather than unlimited. Judicial review enforces constitutional limits; it does not mean courts may replace every policy choice they dislike.

### Conclusion
Constitutionalism turns constitutional text into a system of limited and accountable government. Constitutional supremacy, rights, rule of law, judicial review and democratic responsibility work together to prevent arbitrary power.

## Q4. Is India federal? Discuss the federal structure and centralizing features. (Long answer)

### Introduction
Federalism divides governmental authority constitutionally between at least two levels of government, each having protected functions. India has a federal structure, but it also contains significant centralizing features. The most accurate answer considers both sides rather than describing India as either a perfectly symmetrical federation or a wholly unitary State.

### Federal features
1. **Constitutional distribution of legislative powers:** Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule allocate subjects across Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
2. **Written and supreme Constitution:** Both levels derive authority from the Constitution and are bound by its limits.
3. **Independent judiciary:** Courts resolve constitutional disputes, including disputes over the distribution of powers.
4. **Constitutional amendment safeguards:** Some changes affecting the federal scheme require ratification by at least half of State legislatures under Article 368.
5. **Elected State governments:** States have constitutionally recognized institutions and responsibilities.
6. **Bicameral Parliament:** The Council of States provides a federal element at Union level, although its composition does not make every State equally represented.

### Centralizing features
1. **Residuary legislative power:** Residuary subjects generally lie with Parliament under Article 248 and the relevant constitutional entries.
2. **Union List and concurrent lawmaking:** Parliament has broad authority over Union subjects and may legislate on Concurrent List subjects; Article 254 addresses repugnancy between certain Union and State laws.
3. **Emergency provisions:** National and other constitutionally specified emergencies can alter the normal federal balance.
4. **Article 356:** The Union may, subject to constitutional conditions and judicial review, take action when State constitutional machinery fails.
5. **Parliamentary power in specified circumstances:** The Constitution allows Parliament to legislate on State List subjects in defined situations, including under Articles 249 and 250.
6. **Integrated judiciary and single citizenship:** India has an integrated judicial system and single citizenship.
7. **Governor and other institutional arrangements:** The Constitution gives the Governor a role within State government, with scope and limits governed by constitutional text and case law.

### Judicial approach
In *State of West Bengal v Union of India* (1963), the Supreme Court discussed the nature of the Union and States under the Indian Constitution. In *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994), federalism was treated as a basic feature, and the use of Article 356 was held reviewable by courts. The precise proposition should be tied to the issue and judgment rather than relying on a slogan.

### Evaluation
The Constitution does not divide sovereignty as if the Union and States were independent countries. Both derive their powers from one Constitution. Yet States are not merely administrative agencies: they have constitutionally allocated powers and elected institutions. Centralizing features must be exercised within constitutional limits.

### Conclusion
India is best described as a federation with a strong Union and distinctive unitary features. Its federal character is protected by constitutional distribution, State institutions, judicial interpretation and the basic-structure doctrine, while central powers operate in circumstances and through procedures prescribed by the Constitution.

## Q5. Explain the basic-structure doctrine and its relationship with Article 368. (Long answer)

### Introduction
Article 368 provides the procedure for constitutional amendment. The basic-structure doctrine limits the substantive extent of that power: Parliament may amend the Constitution, but it cannot destroy its basic structure.

### Development
1. **Shankari Prasad v Union of India (1951):** The Court upheld the amendment power in the context before it and treated a constitutional amendment differently from “law” for Article 13 purposes.
2. **Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967):** The Court took a restrictive view of Parliament's ability to amend Fundamental Rights. The decision was later superseded in important respects by constitutional amendment and subsequent doctrine.
3. **Twenty-fourth Amendment (1971):** Parliament amended the Constitution to affirm its constituent power and the amendment process.
4. **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973):** By a narrow majority, the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368 but cannot damage or destroy its basic structure.
5. **Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India (1980):** The Court reaffirmed that limited amending power is itself a basic feature and rejected an unlimited claim of constituent power.
6. **I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu (2007):** Laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after the relevant constitutional cut-off are subject to basic-structure review where they damage Fundamental Rights forming part of the basic structure, under the Court's framework.

### Possible basic features
Case law has identified features such as constitutional supremacy, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review, separation of powers, and the limited nature of the amendment power. There is no single exhaustive list that resolves every future dispute.

### Relationship with Article 13
Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Article 13(4) states that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368. This does not mean amendments are immune from review: the basic-structure doctrine supplies a distinct limitation on the amendment power. Do not apply the ordinary-law test and amendment test as though they were identical.

### Criticism and evaluation
Supporters argue that the doctrine protects constitutional identity against an amendment majority that might abolish democracy, judicial review or other foundational commitments. Critics question whether judges have sufficient textual guidance to identify an unwritten limit and warn of judicial subjectivity. The doctrine's legitimacy is debated, but it is binding Supreme Court law.

### Conclusion
Article 368 enables constitutional change, while the basic-structure doctrine protects constitutional continuity. The doctrine is not a ban on amendment; it prevents amendments that destroy the Constitution's foundational identity.

## Q6. Distinguish Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. (Medium answer)

| Basis | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Part III, Articles 12–35 | Part IV, Articles 36–51 |
| Main purpose | Protect specified rights and constrain public power | Guide the State toward social and economic welfare |
| Enforcement | Enforceable through constitutional remedies, subject to each right's scope and limits | Article 37 says they are not enforceable by courts, but are fundamental in governance |
| Nature | Include civil/political liberties and other constitutional protections | Include social, economic and governance objectives |
| State obligation | Often negative restraints, but some rights also entail positive duties | Primarily guide legislative and executive policy |
| Judicial role | Courts may invalidate incompatible State action and grant remedies | Courts may use them as interpretive context, but cannot treat them as directly enforceable rights merely because they are important |
| Relationship | Must be interpreted in the constitutional scheme | Must be pursued consistently with the Constitution and relevant Fundamental Rights |

### Case law
In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Court held that a law could not prevail over Fundamental Rights merely because it sought to implement a Directive Principle; the constitutional and legislative response later included amendments. In *Minerva Mills* (1980), the Court emphasized the importance of balance between Parts III and IV and held that the Constitution's harmony between them is part of its basic structure.

### Conclusion
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles serve different but complementary purposes. Rights provide enforceable constitutional protections, while Directive Principles guide the State toward social justice. Neither Part should be treated as irrelevant; their relationship must be resolved through constitutional text and binding precedent.

## Q7. Discuss separation of powers and checks and balances under the Indian Constitution. (Medium/long answer)

### Meaning
Separation of powers allocates legislative, executive and judicial functions among different institutions to prevent excessive concentration of authority. Checks and balances allow each institution, within constitutional limits, to restrain or review certain actions of the others.

### Indian constitutional position
India does not adopt a rigid separation in which the branches never overlap. Instead, it has a functional distribution of powers with checks:
- **Legislature:** enacts laws, controls public finance through constitutional procedures, and holds the executive accountable through parliamentary mechanisms.
- **Executive:** administers laws and conducts government within constitutional and statutory authority; the parliamentary executive is responsible to the legislature.
- **Judiciary:** interprets law, resolves disputes and reviews constitutionality within its jurisdiction.
- **Constitutional checks:** judicial review, legislative accountability, prescribed appointment/removal procedures for certain offices, federal distribution of power and constitutional amendment requirements all constrain public power.

### Judicial review and basic structure
Judicial review helps enforce constitutional boundaries, including Fundamental Rights and the distribution of powers. In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the basic-structure doctrine limited the amendment power. Separation of powers and judicial review have been treated as important constitutional principles, although their precise application depends on context.

### Limits
The doctrine does not mean that courts may manage the executive or make policy whenever they disagree with it. Nor can the legislature disregard a binding constitutional judgment. Each institution must act within its constitutional role, and disputes are resolved using the text and precedent.

### Conclusion
The Indian Constitution creates a flexible rather than absolute separation of powers. The combination of institutional roles, judicial review, legislative accountability and federal safeguards aims to prevent arbitrary power while allowing government to function.

## Q8. Explain the significance of *Kesavananda Bharati* and *Minerva Mills*. (Long answer)

### Introduction
*Kesavananda Bharati* and *Minerva Mills* are landmark decisions defining the scope and limits of Parliament's constitutional amendment power and the relationship between constitutional change, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

### A. *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973)
The case considered the extent of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution, against the background of disputes involving property, social reform and constitutional amendments. The Supreme Court held, by a narrow majority, that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368 but cannot destroy its basic structure.

**Significance:**
1. Reconciled the power to amend with constitutional continuity.
2. Established a substantive limit on the amendment power.
3. Recognized the Preamble as part of the Constitution and relevant to interpretation.
4. Laid the foundation for review of amendments that damage foundational constitutional features.

The judgment is lengthy and contains multiple opinions. A careful answer should not attribute every later-recognized basic feature to every judge in the case or claim that the Court produced a single exhaustive list.

### B. *Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India* (1980)
The case challenged provisions of the Forty-second Amendment that attempted to expand Parliament's amending power and broaden the priority of Directive Principles over certain Fundamental Rights. The Court invalidated the relevant provisions to the extent they damaged constitutional limits.

**Significance:**
1. Reaffirmed that Parliament's amending power is limited, not unlimited.
2. Treated limited amending power as part of the basic structure.
3. Emphasized harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights in Part III and Directive Principles in Part IV.
4. Reaffirmed the role of judicial review in maintaining constitutional limits.

### Comparison
- *Kesavananda Bharati* established the basic-structure limitation.
- *Minerva Mills* reinforced the doctrine and applied it to provisions that sought to make the amendment power effectively unlimited and disturb the constitutional balance between Parts III and IV.
- Both preserve the Constitution's identity while allowing amendment and social change.

### Conclusion
Together, the cases establish that constitutional amendment is broad but not unlimited. Parliament can respond to changing social needs, but it cannot use Article 368 to destroy foundational features or eliminate the constitutional limits on its own power.
