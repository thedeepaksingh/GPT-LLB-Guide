## 1. Very short answers (4/5 marks)

### 1. What is constitutionalism?
Constitutionalism is the principle that public power is limited by constitutional rules, rights, procedures and institutional checks. A constitution identifies and structures public authority; constitutionalism requires that authority to be exercised within legal limits and remain accountable. Judicial review, fundamental rights and lawful procedures are common mechanisms supporting constitutionalism.

### 2. What is the significance of the Preamble?
The Preamble expresses the Constitution’s foundational objectives and values, including justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. It is part of the Constitution and can guide interpretation, but it does not ordinarily operate as an independent source of enforceable powers or override operative provisions. *Kesavananda Bharati* is the leading authority on its constitutional significance.

### 3. Explain the basic-structure doctrine.
In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy its basic structure. The doctrine preserves constitutional identity while allowing amendment. The Court has not provided a single exhaustive list covering every possible case.

### 4. Explain Article 12.
Article 12 defines “State” for Part III to include Union and State governments and legislatures, local authorities and other authorities within India or under Government of India control. Courts have developed contextual tests for deciding whether a body is an instrumentality of the State. This matters because many Fundamental Rights constrain State action.

### 5. What is reasonable classification under Article 14?
The traditional test asks whether a classification rests on an intelligible differentia distinguishing those grouped together from others and whether that differentia has a rational relation to the law’s objective. Article 14 doctrine also addresses arbitrariness and has developed beyond a single formula, so the relevant test depends on the issue and precedent.

### 6. Explain Article 19(2).
Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions on speech and expression only on the grounds enumerated in the constitutional text, including sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. A restriction must have legal basis, fall within an authorized ground and satisfy the applicable reasonableness/constitutional test.

### 7. What is Article 21?
Article 21 protects every person from deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. After *Maneka Gandhi*, the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, and Articles 14, 19 and 21 may be read together. The right’s application depends on the facts, precedent and lawful limitations.

### 8. Distinguish Articles 32 and 226.
Article 32 guarantees access to the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”, giving its text wider subject-matter scope. Both have jurisdictional and remedial doctrines; Article 226 is not simply identical to Article 32.

### 9. What is the doctrine of severability?
Where a law contains valid and invalid provisions, a court may invalidate only the unconstitutional part if it can be separated and the remainder can function consistently with legislative intent. The result depends on the statute’s structure, purpose and applicable doctrine.

### 10. What is the right against self-incrimination?
Article 20(3) provides that no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. Case law distinguishes testimonial compulsion from certain forms of physical evidence. The precise application depends on the nature of the evidence, compulsion and procedural context.

### 11. What is the right to privacy in India?
In *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India* (2017), a nine-judge Supreme Court bench unanimously recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right grounded in liberty and dignity. The validity of a particular State intrusion requires further analysis of the applicable constitutional test and facts; privacy is not an unlimited right.

### 12. What is the difference between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles?
Fundamental Rights in Part III are enforceable through constitutional remedies subject to their text and doctrine. Directive Principles in Part IV guide governance and social policy and are not enforceable by courts in the same way. They are both constitutionally significant; *Minerva Mills* illustrates the importance of their relationship and balance.

## Q1. Explain the salient features of the Constitution of India. (Long answer)

### Introduction
The Constitution of India is the supreme legal framework that establishes public institutions, distributes governmental powers, protects rights and sets the principles by which public authority must operate. Its distinctive character lies in combining democratic government with enforceable constitutional limits.

### 1. Written and detailed Constitution
The Constitution sets out the principal institutions, powers, rights and procedures in a comprehensive written text. Its detail reflects India's size, diversity, historical circumstances and need to regulate Union–State relations and social transformation.

### 2. Popular sovereignty and the Preamble
The Preamble begins with “We, the People of India”, locating constitutional authority in the people. It declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and identifies justice, liberty, equality and fraternity as foundational objectives. The words “socialist”, “secular” and “integrity” were added by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976.

### 3. Parliamentary democracy
The Union follows a parliamentary system. The President is the constitutional head, while the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister exercises executive functions under the Constitution and is collectively responsible to the House of the People. The system links executive authority to legislative accountability.

### 4. Federal system with a strong Union
Legislative powers are distributed between the Union and States, notably through the Seventh Schedule. The Constitution also gives the Union significant powers and provides emergency mechanisms. India is therefore commonly described as federal with distinctive centralizing features. In *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994), the Supreme Court discussed federalism and secularism in reviewing the use of Article 356.

### 5. Fundamental Rights
Part III (Articles 12–35) protects specified rights and provides constitutional standards against which State action may be tested. Rights are not all absolute; their scope, restrictions and remedies depend on the text and judicial interpretation.

### 6. Directive Principles of State Policy
Part IV guides the State toward social and economic justice. Directive Principles are not enforceable in the same way as Fundamental Rights, but they are constitutionally important. In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India* (1980), the Court emphasized constitutional balance between Parts III and IV and the limited nature of the amending power.

### 7. Independent judiciary and judicial review
An independent judiciary adjudicates disputes and reviews laws and executive action for constitutional compliance. Judicial review protects constitutional supremacy, while courts must still act within their judicial role and jurisdiction.

### 8. Supremacy of the Constitution and rule of law
Public institutions possess only the powers given or permitted by law. Government action cannot be justified solely by political preference if it violates constitutional requirements. Rule of law, equality and accountable power are central to constitutionalism.

### 9. Amendment procedure subject to basic structure
Article 368 establishes a special amendment procedure. Some amendments require special parliamentary majorities; specified categories also require ratification by at least half of State legislatures. In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament cannot use its amendment power to destroy the Constitution's basic structure.

### 10. Secular and republican character
The State has no constitutionally established national religion and must operate consistently with constitutional religious freedom and equality. A republic has a non-hereditary head of State. The precise constitutional implications of secularism are developed through the text and case law, including *S.R. Bommai*.

### 11. Universal adult suffrage and constitutional bodies
The constitutional framework supports representative government through adult suffrage. Independent or constitutionally protected institutions—including the Election Commission and Comptroller and Auditor General—support electoral integrity and public accountability.

### 12. Emergency provisions and single citizenship
The Constitution provides emergency mechanisms under defined conditions, while their use remains subject to constitutional limits. It also provides for single Indian citizenship rather than separate citizenship of each State.

### Conclusion
The Constitution combines democratic legitimacy, federal distribution, rights, judicial review, social-policy commitments and a structured amendment process. These features must be read together: constitutional democracy requires both effective government and enforceable limits on public power.

**Authorities to mention:** Constitution of India, Preamble, Parts III and IV, Articles 14, 32, 226 and 368; *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973); *Minerva Mills* (1980); *S.R. Bommai* (1994).

## Q2. Discuss the significance and legal status of the Preamble. (Medium/long answer)

### Meaning and text
The Preamble is the introductory statement of constitutional purpose. It records that the Constitution is adopted by the people of India and describes India as a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic. It sets out the objectives of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, including individual dignity and national unity and integrity.

### Legal significance
1. **Expresses constitutional philosophy:** It identifies the values that inform the constitutional order.
2. **Aids interpretation:** Where operative provisions reasonably permit more than one interpretation, the Preamble can help identify the Constitution's purposes. It cannot override clear constitutional text.
3. **Part of the Constitution:** In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court recognized the Preamble as part of the Constitution and treated it as relevant to constitutional interpretation.
4. **Basic-structure relevance:** The Preamble's values help illuminate features that may form part of the Constitution's basic structure. The precise basic-structure analysis comes from the doctrine and case law, not from treating every Preamble word as a separate enforceable right.
5. **Amendability with limits:** The Preamble may be amended through Article 368, but an amendment cannot destroy the basic structure.
6. **Not ordinarily a freestanding cause of action:** A claimant generally must connect a grievance to an operative constitutional provision, legal power or enforceable right. The Preamble does not independently confer every specific remedy.

### Development in case law
In *In re Berubari Union* (1960), the Court expressed an earlier view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. This position was clarified by the larger-bench decision in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), which held that it is part of the Constitution and can aid interpretation.

### Conclusion
The Preamble is neither legally irrelevant nor a substitute for operative Articles. It is a constitutional compass: it explains the Constitution's purposes and assists interpretation, while enforceable claims must be grounded in the constitutional text and applicable doctrine.

## Q3. Explain constitutionalism and the supremacy of the Constitution. (Medium answer)

### Meaning of constitutionalism
Constitutionalism is the principle that public power must be limited, legally justified and accountable. It requires more than the existence of a document called a constitution. A government might possess a written constitution yet lack meaningful constitutionalism if public authorities exercise unchecked power.

### Main elements
- **Limited government:** Every public authority must act within its lawful powers.
- **Rule of law:** Power is exercised under public, knowable legal standards rather than arbitrary will.
- **Fundamental Rights:** Part III protects specified individual and group interests against unconstitutional State action.
- **Separation of functions and checks:** Legislature, executive and judiciary have distinct roles and mechanisms of accountability, though India does not adopt an absolute separation.
- **Judicial review:** Courts can examine the constitutionality of laws and public action.
- **Accountability and democracy:** Elections, legislative responsibility and transparent legal procedures help control public power.
- **Independent adjudication:** Courts must be able to decide disputes without improper interference.

### Supremacy of the Constitution in India
The Constitution is the highest legal framework for State institutions. Ordinary laws and executive actions must conform to it. Articles 13, 32 and 226 support constitutional review and remedies. Constitutional amendments follow Article 368 and are subject to the basic-structure limitation recognized in *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973).

### Important distinction
Parliament is a powerful democratic legislature, but its authority is constitutionally structured rather than unlimited. Judicial review enforces constitutional limits; it does not mean courts may replace every policy choice they dislike.

### Conclusion
Constitutionalism turns constitutional text into a system of limited and accountable government. Constitutional supremacy, rights, rule of law, judicial review and democratic responsibility work together to prevent arbitrary power.

## Q4. Is India federal? Discuss the federal structure and centralizing features. (Long answer)

### Introduction
Federalism divides governmental authority constitutionally between at least two levels of government, each having protected functions. India has a federal structure, but it also contains significant centralizing features. The most accurate answer considers both sides rather than describing India as either a perfectly symmetrical federation or a wholly unitary State.

### Federal features
1. **Constitutional distribution of legislative powers:** Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule allocate subjects across Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
2. **Written and supreme Constitution:** Both levels derive authority from the Constitution and are bound by its limits.
3. **Independent judiciary:** Courts resolve constitutional disputes, including disputes over the distribution of powers.
4. **Constitutional amendment safeguards:** Some changes affecting the federal scheme require ratification by at least half of State legislatures under Article 368.
5. **Elected State governments:** States have constitutionally recognized institutions and responsibilities.
6. **Bicameral Parliament:** The Council of States provides a federal element at Union level, although its composition does not make every State equally represented.

### Centralizing features
1. **Residuary legislative power:** Residuary subjects generally lie with Parliament under Article 248 and the relevant constitutional entries.
2. **Union List and concurrent lawmaking:** Parliament has broad authority over Union subjects and may legislate on Concurrent List subjects; Article 254 addresses repugnancy between certain Union and State laws.
3. **Emergency provisions:** National and other constitutionally specified emergencies can alter the normal federal balance.
4. **Article 356:** The Union may, subject to constitutional conditions and judicial review, take action when State constitutional machinery fails.
5. **Parliamentary power in specified circumstances:** The Constitution allows Parliament to legislate on State List subjects in defined situations, including under Articles 249 and 250.
6. **Integrated judiciary and single citizenship:** India has an integrated judicial system and single citizenship.
7. **Governor and other institutional arrangements:** The Constitution gives the Governor a role within State government, with scope and limits governed by constitutional text and case law.

### Judicial approach
In *State of West Bengal v Union of India* (1963), the Supreme Court discussed the nature of the Union and States under the Indian Constitution. In *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994), federalism was treated as a basic feature, and the use of Article 356 was held reviewable by courts. The precise proposition should be tied to the issue and judgment rather than relying on a slogan.

### Evaluation
The Constitution does not divide sovereignty as if the Union and States were independent countries. Both derive their powers from one Constitution. Yet States are not merely administrative agencies: they have constitutionally allocated powers and elected institutions. Centralizing features must be exercised within constitutional limits.

### Conclusion
India is best described as a federation with a strong Union and distinctive unitary features. Its federal character is protected by constitutional distribution, State institutions, judicial interpretation and the basic-structure doctrine, while central powers operate in circumstances and through procedures prescribed by the Constitution.

## Q5. Explain the basic-structure doctrine and its relationship with Article 368. (Long answer)

### Introduction
Article 368 provides the procedure for constitutional amendment. The basic-structure doctrine limits the substantive extent of that power: Parliament may amend the Constitution, but it cannot destroy its basic structure.

### Development
1. **Shankari Prasad v Union of India (1951):** The Court upheld the amendment power in the context before it and treated a constitutional amendment differently from “law” for Article 13 purposes.
2. **Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967):** The Court took a restrictive view of Parliament's ability to amend Fundamental Rights. The decision was later superseded in important respects by constitutional amendment and subsequent doctrine.
3. **Twenty-fourth Amendment (1971):** Parliament amended the Constitution to affirm its constituent power and the amendment process.
4. **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973):** By a narrow majority, the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368 but cannot damage or destroy its basic structure.
5. **Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India (1980):** The Court reaffirmed that limited amending power is itself a basic feature and rejected an unlimited claim of constituent power.
6. **I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu (2007):** Laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after the relevant constitutional cut-off are subject to basic-structure review where they damage Fundamental Rights forming part of the basic structure, under the Court's framework.

### Possible basic features
Case law has identified features such as constitutional supremacy, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review, separation of powers, and the limited nature of the amendment power. There is no single exhaustive list that resolves every future dispute.

### Relationship with Article 13
Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Article 13(4) states that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368. This does not mean amendments are immune from review: the basic-structure doctrine supplies a distinct limitation on the amendment power. Do not apply the ordinary-law test and amendment test as though they were identical.

### Criticism and evaluation
Supporters argue that the doctrine protects constitutional identity against an amendment majority that might abolish democracy, judicial review or other foundational commitments. Critics question whether judges have sufficient textual guidance to identify an unwritten limit and warn of judicial subjectivity. The doctrine's legitimacy is debated, but it is binding Supreme Court law.

### Conclusion
Article 368 enables constitutional change, while the basic-structure doctrine protects constitutional continuity. The doctrine is not a ban on amendment; it prevents amendments that destroy the Constitution's foundational identity.

## Q6. Distinguish Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. (Medium answer)

| Basis | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Part III, Articles 12–35 | Part IV, Articles 36–51 |
| Main purpose | Protect specified rights and constrain public power | Guide the State toward social and economic welfare |
| Enforcement | Enforceable through constitutional remedies, subject to each right's scope and limits | Article 37 says they are not enforceable by courts, but are fundamental in governance |
| Nature | Include civil/political liberties and other constitutional protections | Include social, economic and governance objectives |
| State obligation | Often negative restraints, but some rights also entail positive duties | Primarily guide legislative and executive policy |
| Judicial role | Courts may invalidate incompatible State action and grant remedies | Courts may use them as interpretive context, but cannot treat them as directly enforceable rights merely because they are important |
| Relationship | Must be interpreted in the constitutional scheme | Must be pursued consistently with the Constitution and relevant Fundamental Rights |

### Case law
In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Court held that a law could not prevail over Fundamental Rights merely because it sought to implement a Directive Principle; the constitutional and legislative response later included amendments. In *Minerva Mills* (1980), the Court emphasized the importance of balance between Parts III and IV and held that the Constitution's harmony between them is part of its basic structure.

### Conclusion
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles serve different but complementary purposes. Rights provide enforceable constitutional protections, while Directive Principles guide the State toward social justice. Neither Part should be treated as irrelevant; their relationship must be resolved through constitutional text and binding precedent.

## Q7. Discuss separation of powers and checks and balances under the Indian Constitution. (Medium/long answer)

### Meaning
Separation of powers allocates legislative, executive and judicial functions among different institutions to prevent excessive concentration of authority. Checks and balances allow each institution, within constitutional limits, to restrain or review certain actions of the others.

### Indian constitutional position
India does not adopt a rigid separation in which the branches never overlap. Instead, it has a functional distribution of powers with checks:
- **Legislature:** enacts laws, controls public finance through constitutional procedures, and holds the executive accountable through parliamentary mechanisms.
- **Executive:** administers laws and conducts government within constitutional and statutory authority; the parliamentary executive is responsible to the legislature.
- **Judiciary:** interprets law, resolves disputes and reviews constitutionality within its jurisdiction.
- **Constitutional checks:** judicial review, legislative accountability, prescribed appointment/removal procedures for certain offices, federal distribution of power and constitutional amendment requirements all constrain public power.

### Judicial review and basic structure
Judicial review helps enforce constitutional boundaries, including Fundamental Rights and the distribution of powers. In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the basic-structure doctrine limited the amendment power. Separation of powers and judicial review have been treated as important constitutional principles, although their precise application depends on context.

### Limits
The doctrine does not mean that courts may manage the executive or make policy whenever they disagree with it. Nor can the legislature disregard a binding constitutional judgment. Each institution must act within its constitutional role, and disputes are resolved using the text and precedent.

### Conclusion
The Indian Constitution creates a flexible rather than absolute separation of powers. The combination of institutional roles, judicial review, legislative accountability and federal safeguards aims to prevent arbitrary power while allowing government to function.

## Q8. Explain the significance of *Kesavananda Bharati* and *Minerva Mills*. (Long answer)

### Introduction
*Kesavananda Bharati* and *Minerva Mills* are landmark decisions defining the scope and limits of Parliament's constitutional amendment power and the relationship between constitutional change, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

### A. *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973)
The case considered the extent of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution, against the background of disputes involving property, social reform and constitutional amendments. The Supreme Court held, by a narrow majority, that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368 but cannot destroy its basic structure.

**Significance:**
1. Reconciled the power to amend with constitutional continuity.
2. Established a substantive limit on the amendment power.
3. Recognized the Preamble as part of the Constitution and relevant to interpretation.
4. Laid the foundation for review of amendments that damage foundational constitutional features.

The judgment is lengthy and contains multiple opinions. A careful answer should not attribute every later-recognized basic feature to every judge in the case or claim that the Court produced a single exhaustive list.

### B. *Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India* (1980)
The case challenged provisions of the Forty-second Amendment that attempted to expand Parliament's amending power and broaden the priority of Directive Principles over certain Fundamental Rights. The Court invalidated the relevant provisions to the extent they damaged constitutional limits.

**Significance:**
1. Reaffirmed that Parliament's amending power is limited, not unlimited.
2. Treated limited amending power as part of the basic structure.
3. Emphasized harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights in Part III and Directive Principles in Part IV.
4. Reaffirmed the role of judicial review in maintaining constitutional limits.

### Comparison
- *Kesavananda Bharati* established the basic-structure limitation.
- *Minerva Mills* reinforced the doctrine and applied it to provisions that sought to make the amendment power effectively unlimited and disturb the constitutional balance between Parts III and IV.
- Both preserve the Constitution's identity while allowing amendment and social change.

### Conclusion
Together, the cases establish that constitutional amendment is broad but not unlimited. Parliament can respond to changing social needs, but it cannot use Article 368 to destroy foundational features or eliminate the constitutional limits on its own power.

## Q1. Explain Article 12 and the tests for determining whether a body is “State”. (Long answer)

Article 12 defines “State” for Part III. It includes the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, local authorities, and other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India. The definition matters because many Fundamental Rights constrain State action.

The Constitution does not give an exhaustive list of every body that qualifies as an “other authority”. Courts have developed a contextual instrumentality/agency analysis. In *R.D. Shetty v International Airport Authority of India* (1979) and *Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib* (1981), the Supreme Court considered factors such as the extent of governmental financial assistance, deep and pervasive control, public functions, State-conferred or protected monopoly, and whether a government department's functions were transferred to the body. The factors are indicators, not a mechanical checklist; no single factor is invariably conclusive.

The legal form of an organization is not alone decisive. A corporation may be an instrumentality of the State, while a body performing some public activity is not automatically State for every purpose. The relevant facts and subsequent case law must be considered.

Article 12 status is important for claims under provisions such as Articles 14 and 16. However, the scope of a particular right and the possibility of horizontal effect must also be considered; it is inaccurate to say every Part III right operates identically only against State actors.

**Conclusion:** Article 12 identifies the public bodies against which Part III principally operates. Courts use a contextual, functional and institutional analysis to determine whether an entity is an instrumentality of the State.

## Q2. Explain Article 13 and the doctrines of severability and eclipse. (Medium/long answer)

Article 13 deals with laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Article 13(1) addresses pre-Constitution laws to the extent of inconsistency with Part III. Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights, with the consequence prescribed by the Article to the extent of contravention.

**Severability** allows a court to invalidate the unconstitutional part of a law while preserving the remainder if the provisions are separable and the remaining law can function consistently with legislative intent. Courts consider the structure, purpose and operation of the enactment rather than severing mechanically.

The **eclipse doctrine** is associated with certain pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with a Fundamental Right. Such a law may be inoperative to the extent of inconsistency rather than erased for every purpose; its precise operation depends on the right, the law and precedent. Avoid stating that every unconstitutional law is simply “dead” in all circumstances.

Article 13(4) provides that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368. This does not make constitutional amendments immune from review: the basic-structure doctrine limits the amending power. Ordinary-law review and constitutional-amendment review are distinct.

**Conclusion:** Article 13 supports constitutional supremacy and review of ordinary laws, while severability and eclipse address different consequences of inconsistency. Always identify whether the law predates the Constitution and whether the challenge is to an ordinary law or an amendment.

## Q3. Explain Article 14 and the doctrine of reasonable classification. (Long answer)

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to any person within its scope. Equality before law expresses the rejection of arbitrary privilege; equal protection requires like cases to be treated alike while permitting relevant distinctions.

The traditional reasonable-classification test has two limbs:
1. The classification must rest on an **intelligible differentia** distinguishing those grouped together from those left out.
2. The differentia must have a **rational relation** to the objective sought by the law.

Both limbs must be satisfied. A classification cannot be justified merely by attaching a label to a group; its distinction must relate to the law's legitimate purpose.

Article 14 jurisprudence also developed an arbitrariness line. In *E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu* (1974), the Court linked equality and arbitrariness in the context of State action. Later decisions apply Article 14 in varied contexts, so not every case is resolved solely by repeating the two-part classification formula.

A strong answer identifies the State action, the persons compared, the objective of the measure, the basis for distinction and the relevant standard of review. It should consider whether the classification is genuine and whether the means are constitutionally justified.

**Conclusion:** Article 14 does not prohibit every classification; it prohibits constitutionally impermissible inequality and arbitrary State action. The applicable analysis depends on the facts and doctrinal context.

## Q4. Discuss the freedoms guaranteed by Article 19 and their reasonable restrictions. (Long answer)

Article 19(1) guarantees specified freedoms to citizens: speech and expression; peaceful assembly without arms; association, unions and co-operative societies; movement throughout India; residence and settlement; and profession, occupation, trade or business. The former property-related freedom is no longer in Article 19; property is protected principally under Article 300A.

These freedoms are not absolute. Each relevant clause contains its own restriction grounds. Article 19(2), for example, permits reasonable restrictions on speech and expression on the grounds listed in that clause, including sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. The grounds cannot simply be transferred to other Article 19 freedoms.

A constitutional challenge should be analysed in stages:
1. Identify the precise freedom and whether the claimant is a citizen.
2. Identify the impugned law or State action and its legal basis.
3. Identify the exact restriction clause and authorized ground.
4. Apply the relevant reasonableness/proportionality doctrine and precedent.
5. Consider evidence, scope, safeguards and remedy.

In *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015), the Supreme Court struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, addressing its vagueness and chilling effect on expression. *Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India* (2020) considered restrictions affecting internet-based expression and trade, including procedural requirements for review and publication. These cases do not establish that every restriction on online speech or internet access is automatically invalid.

**Conclusion:** Article 19 protects core democratic freedoms while permitting only restrictions authorized by the constitutional text and supported by the applicable legal test. The exact clause is essential to a correct answer.

## Q5. Explain Article 20 and its three protections. (Medium/long answer)

Article 20 protects every person in criminal-law contexts through three safeguards:
- **Article 20(1):** protects against conviction for an act that was not an offence when done and against a greater penalty than the one applicable at the time, according to the constitutional text.
- **Article 20(2):** protects against being prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once, in the terms of the constitutional guarantee.
- **Article 20(3):** provides that a person accused of an offence cannot be compelled to be a witness against himself.

The protections must be analysed separately. Double jeopardy under Article 20(2) has a constitutional formulation that should not automatically be equated with every statutory rule about successive proceedings. Article 20(3) concerns testimonial compulsion; the Supreme Court has distinguished compelled testimony from certain forms of physical evidence. *State of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad* and *Selvi v State of Karnataka* are relevant authorities, but the facts and precise holdings must be stated carefully.

**Conclusion:** Article 20 protects fairness and personal security in criminal proceedings through distinct guarantees against retrospective penal consequences, repeated prosecution and compelled self-incrimination.

## Q6. Explain the constitutional safeguards relating to arrest and preventive detention under Article 22. (Medium/long answer)

Article 22 contains safeguards for persons arrested or detained, but its clauses distinguish ordinary arrest from preventive detention. For ordinary arrest, the Constitution generally requires that the person be informed of the grounds of arrest, be allowed to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, and be produced before the nearest magistrate within the constitutionally prescribed period, subject to the text and exceptions. Detention beyond that period requires lawful authority.

Article 22 contains separate provisions for preventive detention, including safeguards and exceptions that differ from ordinary arrest. A strong answer must identify the relevant clause and not claim that every ordinary-arrest safeguard applies identically to preventive detention.

The constitutional text must also be distinguished from statutory procedure. For current matters, check whether the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 or a savings/transitional rule applies to the facts. *D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* is a major authority on arrest and custodial safeguards, but its propositions should be read alongside current law.

**Conclusion:** Article 22 sets constitutional boundaries for arrest and detention. The correct analysis identifies the type of detention, the applicable clause, the facts and the current procedural law.

## Q7. Discuss freedom of religion under Articles 25–28. (Long answer)

Articles 25–28 protect freedom of conscience and aspects of religious practice while allowing specified constitutional regulation.

**Article 25** protects freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other provisions of Part III. The Article also permits specified regulation of secular activities associated with religious practice and social-reform measures under its text.

**Article 26** protects the rights of religious denominations to establish institutions, manage affairs in matters of religion, own/acquire property and administer such property according to law, subject to constitutional limits.

**Article 27** addresses compulsion to pay taxes specifically appropriated for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion, according to the wording of the provision.

**Article 28** regulates religious instruction or worship in specified educational institutions. The result depends on the institution's constitutional category and the text of the clause.

Courts must distinguish belief, religious practice, denominational autonomy, secular administration and public regulation. *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar* (Shirur Mutt, 1954) is a foundational authority. *Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977) considered the meaning of propagation under Article 25. The “essential religious practices” doctrine is contested and evolving, so it should not be presented as a universally simple test.

**Conclusion:** Articles 25–28 protect religious liberty but balance it with express constitutional limits and other rights. The answer must apply the exact Article and relevant case law to the claimed practice and State action.

## Q8. Explain cultural and educational rights under Articles 29–30. (Medium/long answer)

Article 29 protects specified cultural interests. Article 29(1) provides that any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it. Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them, within the provision's wording.

Article 30 protects religious and linguistic minorities' right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. This supports cultural and educational autonomy, while allowing constitutionally permissible regulation to maintain academic standards, fairness and institutional accountability. The scope of regulation must not destroy the protected right.

The provisions are related but not identical. Article 29(1) is framed for any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture; Article 30 specifically protects minority educational institutions. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka* (2002) is a leading decision on minority educational institutions and the regulatory framework, though its detailed propositions must be read in the full judgment.

**Conclusion:** Articles 29–30 protect cultural diversity and minority educational autonomy while allowing regulation consistent with the constitutional guarantee.

## Q9. Discuss the writ jurisdiction under Articles 32 and 226. (Long answer)

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights and empowers it to issue appropriate directions, orders or writs. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue directions, orders or writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”. The textual reach of Article 226 is therefore broader, although both jurisdictions are governed by constitutional requirements and judicial doctrine.

The five traditional writs are:
- **Habeas corpus:** tests the legality of detention and may secure release where detention is unlawful.
- **Mandamus:** directs performance of a public or legal duty, subject to limits on the respondent and duty.
- **Prohibition:** restrains a lower court or tribunal from proceeding beyond its jurisdiction before the proceedings conclude.
- **Certiorari:** quashes a decision or order for jurisdictional error or other recognized legal defects.
- **Quo warranto:** challenges the legal authority by which a person holds a public office.

The appropriate writ depends on the nature of the right, respondent, duty, jurisdiction and stage of proceedings. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right but is focused on enforcement of Fundamental Rights; Article 226 also extends to other legal purposes. High Courts may consider alternative remedies and other discretionary principles, subject to established exceptions.

**Conclusion:** Articles 32 and 226 are central constitutional remedies. A good answer states their different textual scope and explains the relevant writ in relation to the facts.

## Q10. Discuss the development of Article 21 from *A.K. Gopalan* to *Maneka Gandhi* and *Puttaswamy*. (Long answer)

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Its interpretation has expanded through constitutional adjudication.

In *A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras* (1950), the Supreme Court adopted a more compartmentalized approach to the different Fundamental Rights and considered the validity of preventive detention under the constitutional framework then before it. Later jurisprudence moved away from treating the rights as wholly isolated.

In *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978), the Court interpreted Article 21's procedure requirement to demand fairness, justice and reasonableness, and read Articles 14, 19 and 21 in an interconnected manner where applicable. State action affecting liberty therefore cannot be defended merely by pointing to a procedure in formal terms; the procedure must meet constitutional standards.

Later decisions connected life and liberty with dignity and other protected interests. *Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) discussed livelihood in relation to life, while recognizing limits in the context of public land. In *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India* (2017), a nine-judge bench recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right grounded in liberty and dignity.

Article 21 is broad but not unlimited. A claimant must identify the State action, the precise interest, the applicable doctrine and any lawful limitations. Later cases and current statutes may affect the answer.

**Conclusion:** Article 21 developed from a narrower procedural guarantee into a central protection of liberty and dignity, interpreted through fairness and the broader constitutional scheme.

## Q11. Explain the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. (Medium/long answer)

Fundamental Rights appear in Part III and are enforceable through constitutional remedies, subject to their text and limitations. Directive Principles appear in Part IV and guide the State in making laws and policies to advance social and economic welfare; Article 37 states that they are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in governance.

The two Parts have different legal roles but are not enemies. Rights protect individual and group interests and constrain public power, while Directive Principles articulate constitutional objectives that may guide legislation, policy and interpretation. The challenge is to pursue social justice without violating enforceable constitutional guarantees.

In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Court held that a Directive Principle could not override an inconsistent Fundamental Right under the constitutional position then applicable; later constitutional amendments affected the framework for affirmative action. In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India* (1980), the Court emphasized the importance of harmony and balance between Parts III and IV and invalidated provisions that damaged the limited nature of the amending power and constitutional balance.

**Conclusion:** Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are complementary elements of the constitutional scheme. Rights remain enforceable according to their terms, while Directive Principles guide governance and must be pursued within constitutional limits.

## Q12. Explain judicial review as an element of constitutionalism. (Medium/long answer)

Judicial review is the authority of courts to determine whether laws and public actions comply with the Constitution. It gives practical effect to constitutional supremacy by allowing courts to grant remedies where public power exceeds constitutional limits.

Articles 13, 32 and 226 are important parts of the framework for reviewing ordinary laws and enforcing Fundamental Rights. Judicial review also operates in the context of federal distribution and constitutional amendments, although amendment review under Article 368 and the basic-structure doctrine differs from ordinary-law review.

Judicial review supports rule of law, rights protection and institutional accountability. It can require government to justify its action under the correct legal standard. However, courts must act within jurisdiction, apply precedent and explain their reasoning; judicial review is not a general authority to replace every policy choice with a judicial preference.

*Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973) established the basic-structure limitation on the amendment power. *Minerva Mills* (1980) reaffirmed limited amending power and constitutional balance. These cases illustrate review of constitutional amendments, a distinct question from whether an ordinary statute violates a Fundamental Right.

**Conclusion:** Judicial review is a central safeguard of constitutionalism because it makes constitutional limits legally enforceable, while remaining subject to the Constitution's allocation of institutional roles.

## Ten-mark answers

### Q1. Salient features of the Constitution of India
The Constitution is a detailed written framework for institutions, powers, rights and public responsibilities. Its salient features include popular sovereignty expressed in the Preamble; parliamentary democracy; a federal distribution of powers with a strong Union; Fundamental Rights; Directive Principles; an independent judiciary and judicial review; constitutional supremacy and rule of law; adult suffrage; single citizenship; emergency provisions; and a special amendment procedure.

These features operate together. Fundamental Rights constrain public power, while Directive Principles guide social and economic policy. Federalism distributes authority, while emergency and concurrent powers permit central action in defined circumstances. Article 368 allows amendment but, under *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), cannot be used to destroy the basic structure. *Minerva Mills* (1980) reinforces limited amending power and balance between Parts III and IV.

The Constitution is therefore neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible, and neither purely federal nor wholly unitary. Its distinctive character is a democratic constitutional order combining effective government with enforceable limits.

### Q2. Legal status of the Preamble
The Preamble states the Constitution's foundational objectives: justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, and identifies India as a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic. It expresses popular sovereignty and guides constitutional interpretation.

In *In re Berubari Union* (1960), the Court expressed an earlier view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) clarified that it is part of the Constitution and relevant to interpretation. The Preamble may illuminate constitutional values and basic-structure analysis, but it cannot override clear operative text or ordinarily provide a freestanding cause of action by itself. It can be amended under Article 368, subject to the basic-structure limit.

### Q3. Federalism with a strong Union
India has federal features: a written and supreme Constitution, distribution of legislative powers under Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule, elected State institutions, an independent judiciary and special amendment safeguards for specified federal provisions. Centralizing features include residuary power, Parliament's concurrent and exceptional State List powers, emergency provisions, Article 356 and single citizenship.

In *State of West Bengal v Union of India* (1963), the Court discussed the constitutional relationship between the Union and States. *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994) treated federalism as a basic feature and affirmed judicial review of Article 356 action. India is best described as a federation with a strong Union, not as a collection of sovereign States or a wholly unitary system. Central powers remain subject to constitutional conditions and judicial review.

### Q4. Basic structure: justification and limits
Article 368 provides the constitutional amendment procedure. In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure. The doctrine protects constitutional identity while allowing legal and political adaptation.

Features recognized in different judgments include constitutional supremacy, democracy, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review and limited amending power. There is no exhaustive list that automatically decides every case. *Minerva Mills* (1980) held that limited amending power is itself basic and emphasized the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. *I.R. Coelho* (2007) applied basic-structure review to certain Ninth Schedule insertions made after the relevant cut-off.

The doctrine is defended as a safeguard against a temporary parliamentary supermajority abolishing foundational commitments. Critics question the doctrine's textual precision and the risk of judicial subjectivity. It is nevertheless binding constitutional doctrine. It does not prohibit amendment; it prohibits destruction of the Constitution's basic structure.

### Q5. Article 12 and State instrumentality
Article 12 includes Union and State governments and legislatures, local authorities and “other authorities” within its wording. Courts interpret “other authorities” contextually to determine whether an entity is an instrumentality or agency of the State. *R.D. Shetty v International Airport Authority of India* (1979) and *Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib* (1981) identify factors such as government ownership and financial support, deep and pervasive control, public functions, monopoly status and transfer of governmental functions.

No single factor is invariably conclusive. Government shareholding or public importance alone does not automatically make every entity “State”; the institutional relationship and facts matter. Article 12 status can permit claims under provisions such as Articles 14 and 16, but the scope of the particular right must also be established.

### Q6. Article 14: classification and arbitrariness
Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws to any person within its scope. The traditional classification test requires an intelligible differentia and a rational relation between that distinction and the law's objective. Both limbs must be satisfied.

Article 14 doctrine also addresses arbitrary State action. *E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu* (1974) linked equality and arbitrariness in its context. The applicable test depends on the kind of State action and the relevant precedent; not every dispute is resolved by reciting only the two-part classification formula. A proper answer identifies the comparator, the classification, the purpose, the evidence supporting the distinction and the applicable constitutional standard.

### Q7. Article 19 freedoms and limitations
Article 19(1) protects specified freedoms of citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly without arms, association, movement, residence/settlement and profession/occupation/trade/business. The former property-related freedom is no longer included in Article 19.

Each freedom has its own constitutional limitation clause. Article 19(2), for example, governs restrictions on speech and lists its authorized grounds; those grounds cannot be freely imported into clauses governing other freedoms. A challenge should identify the claimant's citizenship, the precise freedom, the legal basis for the restriction, the exact permitted ground, and the applicable reasonableness or proportionality analysis. *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015) is a major speech case involving Section 66A of the Information Technology Act; its holding should not be generalized to every speech restriction.

### Q8. Article 21: doctrinal development
Article 21 provides that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. *A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras* (1950) adopted a more compartmentalized approach to Fundamental Rights. *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978) required a procedure affecting life or liberty to be fair, just and reasonable and read Articles 14, 19 and 21 in an interconnected manner where applicable. *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* (2017) recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right grounded in liberty and dignity.

Article 21 is broad but not unlimited. Identify the State action, the interest affected, the applicable procedure and constitutional test, and any lawful limitations. Current statutes and later judgments must be checked for the facts at issue.

### Q9. Article 20 protections
Article 20 contains three distinct protections: no retrospective criminal conviction or greater penalty in the terms of Article 20(1); protection against prosecution and punishment for the same offence under Article 20(2); and protection against compelled self-incrimination under Article 20(3). Each clause has its own elements. Do not equate the constitutional double-jeopardy clause with every statutory rule about successive proceedings. *State of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad* and *Selvi v State of Karnataka* are relevant to testimonial compulsion and forms of evidence; state their precise holdings only after checking the judgments.

### Q10. Article 22 and arrest/preventive detention
For ordinary arrest, Article 22 generally provides that the person be informed of grounds, be allowed to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner, and be produced before a magistrate within the constitutionally prescribed period, subject to textual exceptions. Preventive detention is treated in separate clauses with different safeguards and exceptions. Do not apply every ordinary-arrest safeguard identically to preventive detention.

The constitutional text must be distinguished from the procedural statute in force. For contemporary facts, check the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and any applicable savings or transitional provisions. *D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* is an important authority on arrest and custodial safeguards, read with current law.

### Q11. Articles 25–28: freedom of religion
Article 25 protects freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other provisions of Part III, and permits specified regulation of secular activity and social reform. Article 26 protects specified rights of religious denominations, subject to constitutional limits. Article 27 addresses taxes specifically appropriated for promoting or maintaining a particular religion. Article 28 regulates religious instruction or worship in specified educational institutions.

The analysis must distinguish belief, religious practice, denominational autonomy, secular administration and public regulation. *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar* (Shirur Mutt, 1954) is foundational; *Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977) considered propagation. The essential-religious-practices doctrine is contested and must not be reduced to an automatic test.

### Q12. Articles 29–30: cultural and educational rights
Article 29(1) protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it. Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on specified grounds only. Article 30 protects religious and linguistic minorities' right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

The provisions are related but distinct: Article 29(1) is framed for any qualifying section of citizens, while Article 30 specifically protects minority educational institutions. Regulation may be permitted for standards and proper administration, but cannot destroy the protected right. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka* (2002) is a leading authority; detailed propositions should be checked against the judgment.

### Q13. Articles 32 and 226
Article 32 guarantees access to the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”, giving it broader textual subject-matter scope. Both remain subject to jurisdictional rules and remedial doctrine.

Habeas corpus tests legality of detention; mandamus directs performance of a public/legal duty; prohibition restrains a lower court or tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction before completion; certiorari quashes decisions for recognized legal defects; quo warranto tests entitlement to public office. Select the writ based on the right, respondent, duty, jurisdiction and stage of proceedings.

### Q14. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
Fundamental Rights in Part III are enforceable according to their terms and limitations. Directive Principles in Part IV guide the State toward social and economic welfare; Article 37 says they are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in governance. In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), a Directive Principle could not override an inconsistent Fundamental Right under the constitutional position then applicable. *Minerva Mills* (1980) emphasized harmony and balance between Parts III and IV. The two Parts have different roles but must be read within the whole constitutional scheme.

### Q15. Judicial review and constitutionalism
Judicial review enables courts to examine whether laws and public action comply with the Constitution. Articles 13, 32 and 226 support review of ordinary laws and enforcement of Fundamental Rights; review of constitutional amendments under Article 368 involves the distinct basic-structure doctrine. Judicial review protects constitutional supremacy, rights and federal limits, but courts must remain within jurisdiction, apply precedent and provide legal reasons. It is not a general power to substitute judicial policy preferences for every legislative or executive choice.

## Worked problem answers

### Problem A. Speech banned as “offensive” without a stated legal basis
**Issue:** Whether the ban violates Article 19(1)(a).  
**Rule:** Article 19(2) permits restrictions only on its enumerated grounds and subject to constitutional requirements. Offensiveness by itself is not a freestanding Article 19(2) ground.  
**Application:** The authority has not identified a statutory basis or a permitted ground. On the facts given, the ban appears constitutionally vulnerable. The State would need to identify valid legal authority, evidence connecting the restriction to an authorized ground, and compliance with the applicable reasonableness/proportionality standard.  
**Conclusion:** The ban is likely invalid on the stated facts, subject to any additional lawful basis and evidence.

### Problem B. Government-controlled company arbitrarily denies a benefit
**Issue:** Whether the entity is “State” under Article 12 and whether the denial violates Article 14.  
**Rule:** Apply the contextual instrumentality/agency factors in *R.D. Shetty* and *Ajay Hasia*; no single factor automatically decides the issue. If Article 12 applies, State action must satisfy Article 14.  
**Application:** Establish ownership, funding, depth of governmental control, public function, monopoly and whether governmental functions were transferred. If the entity qualifies and the benefit was denied arbitrarily or through unequal criteria, Article 14 may be engaged. More facts about the entity and decision process are needed.  
**Conclusion:** A claim may succeed if Article 12 status and arbitrary State action are established; government connection alone is insufficient.

### Problem C. Passport/liberty decision made without fair procedure
**Issue:** Whether the decision violates Article 21 and potentially Article 14.  
**Rule:** *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* requires procedure affecting life or personal liberty to be fair, just and reasonable; Articles 14, 19 and 21 may be read together where applicable. Statutory requirements and hearing obligations depend on the governing law and facts.  
**Application:** Identify the statutory power, reasons, notice/hearing requirements, urgency or exceptions, and whether the person had an effective opportunity to challenge the decision. A bare assertion of procedure is not enough if the procedure is arbitrary.  
**Conclusion:** The decision may be challenged if the procedure or exercise of power fails constitutional and statutory standards; remedy depends on the record and applicable law.

### Problem D. Arrested person not produced before a magistrate within the prescribed period
**Issue:** Whether Article 22's ordinary-arrest safeguard has been breached.  
**Rule:** Article 22 contains a constitutional requirement for production before the nearest magistrate within the prescribed period, subject to constitutional text and exceptions. Preventive detention is a separate category. Current procedural legislation must also be checked.  
**Application:** Establish the time of arrest, custody, any excluded journey time under the applicable rule, legal basis for detention and whether an exception applies. If no lawful exception explains the delay, detention is constitutionally vulnerable.  
**Conclusion:** On the assumed facts, the failure is likely unlawful and may support prompt judicial relief; the exact remedy depends on the record and current procedure.

### Problem E. Law regulates a claimed religious practice
**Issue:** Whether the measure infringes Article 25 or Article 26, and whether a constitutional limitation applies.  
**Rule:** Article 25 is subject to public order, morality, health and other Part III provisions and includes specified regulatory/social-reform powers. Article 26 protects denominational rights subject to constitutional limits.  
**Application:** Identify the precise practice and evidence, whether the claim concerns belief or conduct, whether it is a denominational matter, whether the regulated activity is secular, and which express limitation or other right applies. Consider relevant precedent carefully; “essential religious practice” analysis is contested and context-sensitive.  
**Conclusion:** Neither religious invocation nor State regulation is automatically decisive. Validity turns on the exact right, constitutional text, evidence, purpose and applicable precedent.
