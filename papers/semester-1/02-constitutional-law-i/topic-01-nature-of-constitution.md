## 1. Meaning of a Constitution
A constitution is the foundational legal and political framework that establishes public institutions, allocates powers, regulates how power is exercised, and states principles and rights that constrain government. It is not merely a description of government; in a constitutional democracy it is also a higher-law framework against which ordinary public action may be tested.

**Constitutionalism** is the idea that public power must be limited, justified and exercised under law. A country may possess a document called a constitution while still falling short of constitutionalism if power is effectively unchecked.

## 2. Sources and historical background
The Constitution of India emerged from the Constituent Assembly’s work and came into force on 26 January 1950. Its background includes the independence movement, demands for representative government and rights, colonial constitutional developments, and comparative constitutional experience. The Constitution draws on multiple models but must be understood as an Indian constitutional settlement rather than a simple copy of any foreign constitution.

Useful historical milestones:
- Government of India Act 1935: an important institutional antecedent, but not the source of the Constitution’s ultimate authority.
- Constituent Assembly debates: useful for understanding choices and competing views, though the adopted constitutional text controls.
- Adoption: 26 November 1949.
- Commencement: 26 January 1950, with certain provisions having earlier commencement under the constitutional text.
Use the official text and authoritative history sources for exact claims.

## 3. Salient features
Commonly identified features include:
1. **Written and detailed Constitution:** sets out institutions, powers, rights and procedures in a comprehensive text.
2. **Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic:** terms appear in the Preamble; “socialist”, “secular” and “integrity” were added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976. The constitutional meaning of each term must be explained, not merely listed.
3. **Parliamentary system:** executive government is responsible to the elected legislature, subject to constitutional design at Union and State levels.
4. **Federal structure with a strong Union:** powers are distributed between Union and States, but the Constitution also contains unitary features and emergency provisions. “Federal” should not be treated as meaning that every power is equally divided.
5. **Fundamental Rights:** Part III protects specified rights and provides constitutional limits on State action.
6. **Directive Principles of State Policy:** Part IV guides governance and social policy; they are not directly enforceable in court in the same way as Fundamental Rights, but are constitutionally important.
7. **Independent judiciary and judicial review:** courts can review public action against constitutional requirements within their jurisdiction.
8. **Supremacy of the Constitution:** public authorities must act within constitutional authority.
9. **Universal adult suffrage:** democratic participation is foundational.
10. **Single citizenship:** the constitutional system provides Indian citizenship rather than separate State citizenship.
11. **Amendment procedure with limits:** Article 368 provides the constitutional amendment procedure; the basic-structure doctrine limits the amending power.
12. **Emergency provisions:** enable exceptional responses under specified constitutional conditions, while raising questions about safeguards and federal balance.
13. **Independent constitutional bodies:** institutions such as the Election Commission and Comptroller and Auditor General support constitutional governance.

Feature lists are not substitutes for analysis. For each feature, give a constitutional basis, explain its purpose, and note any limit or tension.

## 4. The Preamble
The Preamble declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and identifies justice, liberty, equality and fraternity as constitutional objectives.

### Legal significance
- It helps explain the Constitution’s purposes and may guide interpretation where the text admits more than one reading.
- It is part of the Constitution, as recognized in *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973).
- It is not ordinarily a freestanding source of enforceable powers or an independent cause of action detached from operative provisions.
- The Preamble does not override clear constitutional text.
- The Preamble can be amended under Article 368, subject to the basic-structure limitation.

### Values
- **Justice:** social, economic and political.
- **Liberty:** thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
- **Equality:** status and opportunity.
- **Fraternity:** dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.
An answer should connect these terms with operative provisions rather than assume the Preamble itself gives every specific remedy.

## 5. Federalism and parliamentary government
India distributes legislative powers through the constitutional scheme, including the Seventh Schedule’s Union, State and Concurrent Lists. The distribution coexists with Union powers and mechanisms for exceptional circumstances. The Supreme Court has treated federalism as a basic feature in its constitutional jurisprudence.

The parliamentary system links the executive to the confidence and responsibility of the legislature. It differs from a presidential system in which executive tenure and authority are structured differently. In India, the President is the constitutional head at Union level, while the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister exercises executive functions under the constitutional scheme.

## 6. Separation of powers and checks
India does not adopt a strict, complete separation in which each branch has no overlap. Instead, functions are distributed and each branch is subject to constitutional checks. Courts adjudicate, legislatures enact laws, and the executive administers them, but the Constitution allows some overlap and establishes mechanisms of accountability. Judicial review does not make courts the general policy-making branch; it allows them to enforce constitutional limits.

## 7. Amendment and basic structure
Article 368 specifies a special procedure for constitutional amendment. Some amendments require special majorities in Parliament; specified changes also require ratification by at least half of State legislatures. Do not state that every constitutional amendment requires State ratification.

In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225, the Supreme Court held that Parliament’s amendment power cannot be used to destroy the Constitution’s basic structure. There is no single exhaustive list that resolves every future question; features identified in cases include constitutional supremacy, rule of law, judicial review, secularism, federalism and democratic government, depending on context.

**Distinguish:** an ordinary law can be invalid for violating the Constitution; a constitutional amendment is reviewed under the Article 368 framework and basic-structure doctrine. The tests are related but not identical.

## 8. Citizenship — overview only
Part II (Articles 5–11) addresses citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution and Parliament’s power to regulate citizenship by law. Do not try to infer present-day citizenship eligibility from Articles 5–11 alone; current questions may require the Citizenship Act, 1955, its amendments, rules and current judgments. Verify the latest official law for any live citizenship issue.

## 9. Leading case illustrations
- **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala**, (1973) 4 SCC 225 — basic structure and limits on Article 368 amendment power.
- **Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India**, (1980) 3 SCC 625 — limited amending power and constitutional balance, including between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
- **S.R. Bommai v Union of India**, (1994) 3 SCC 1 — federalism, secularism and judicial review in the Article 356 context.
- **I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu**, (2007) 2 SCC 1 — review of laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after the relevant date under the basic-structure framework.
- **In re: Berubari Union**, AIR 1960 SC 845 — historical position on the Preamble, later clarified by *Kesavananda Bharati*. Explain the development rather than citing *Berubari* as the final position.

Check the full judgments before quoting passages or attributing a specific feature to a specific opinion.

## 10. Model 20-mark answer
### Q. Discuss the salient features of the Constitution of India.
**Introduction.** The Constitution of India is the supreme legal framework for public institutions, distribution of powers, fundamental rights and constitutional governance. It combines democratic government with legal limits on public authority.

**Written and detailed text.** It sets out institutions, powers, rights and procedures in a comprehensive document, providing a common legal reference for public authorities and courts.

**Democratic and parliamentary system.** The Constitution establishes representative government and a parliamentary executive accountable through the legislature. Universal adult suffrage supports political equality.

**Federal structure.** Legislative powers are distributed between Union and States, while the constitutional design also gives the Union significant powers. The structure must be described as federal with distinctive centralizing features, not as a perfectly symmetrical federation.

**Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.** Part III protects specified rights; Part IV guides social and economic governance. *Minerva Mills* illustrates the importance of constitutional balance rather than treating either part as wholly irrelevant to the other.

**Independent judiciary and judicial review.** Courts can examine whether laws and public action comply with the Constitution. Judicial review is a means of enforcing constitutional limits, not an unlimited power to replace every political choice.

**Secular, sovereign and republican character.** The Preamble states the Constitution’s political and normative commitments. In *S.R. Bommai*, the Court discussed secularism and federalism in the constitutional context.

**Amendment with limits.** Article 368 permits constitutional change through specified procedures. *Kesavananda Bharati* establishes that the power cannot be used to destroy the Constitution’s basic structure.

**Conclusion.** The Constitution combines democratic authority with rights, judicial review, federal arrangements and an amendment mechanism. Its distinctive character lies in the interaction of these features, including the need to reconcile majority rule with constitutional limits.
