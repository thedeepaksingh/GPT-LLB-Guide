# Topic 2 — Fundamental Rights (Part III)

## 1. Framework and purpose
Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12–35) protects specified rights and limits governmental power. Fundamental Rights are not all absolute; restrictions and exceptions depend on the text of each right and the applicable judicial test. The remedy and burden of justification vary with the right and facts.

## 2. Article 12 — “State”
Article 12 defines “State” for Part III to include the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, and local or other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Courts have developed tests to determine whether a body is an “authority” or instrumentality of the State. Relevant cases include *Rajasthan Electricity Board v Mohan Lal* (1967), *Sukhdev Singh v Bhagatram* (1975), *R.D. Shetty v International Airport Authority of India* (1979), and *Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib* (1981). These decisions examine factors such as governmental control, public functions, financial support and institutional character; no single factor should be presented as automatically decisive in every case.

Article 12 is crucial because many Fundamental Rights are primarily enforceable against the State. Some rights/doctrines have horizontal effects or impose duties affecting private conduct; avoid the blanket claim that every Part III right operates only against government or that every private body is automatically State.

## 3. Article 13 — laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights
Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Part III. In broad terms, pre-Constitution laws are void to the extent of inconsistency, and the State may not make laws taking away or abridging Fundamental Rights. The precise application depends on the type and timing of law, the doctrine invoked and relevant precedent.

Important doctrines:
- **Severability:** an invalid part may be severed if it can be separated and the remainder can operate consistently with legislative intent.
- **Eclipse:** certain pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with a Fundamental Right may be treated as eclipsed to the extent of inconsistency, rather than simply erased for every purpose. The doctrine’s operation is technical and should be explained with relevant authority.
- **Judicial review:** courts determine constitutional compatibility within their jurisdiction.

Article 13(4) states that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368; the basic-structure doctrine separately limits the amending power. Do not say that ordinary laws and constitutional amendments are reviewed under exactly the same test.

## 4. Equality rights — Articles 14–18
### Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws
Article 14 applies to “any person”, not only citizens. It prohibits arbitrary State action and permits reasonable classification if the classification is founded on an intelligible differentia and that differentia has a rational relation to the object sought to be achieved. Modern Article 14 doctrine also addresses arbitrariness; do not reduce every equality case to the two-part classification test without considering the relevant line of authority.

### Article 15: prohibition of discrimination
Article 15 prohibits specified forms of discrimination by the State against citizens on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them, subject to constitutional text and clauses permitting specified special provisions. Article 15(2) also addresses access to specified public places and facilities in its terms. Articles 15(3), 15(4), 15(5) and 15(6) contain enabling provisions for specified categories of special provisions, including women and children, socially and educationally backward classes, SC/ST and economically weaker sections, subject to the precise clauses and applicable case law.

### Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment
Article 16 protects equality of opportunity for citizens in public employment and prohibits specified discrimination, while permitting constitutionally authorized reservation and related provisions. The conditions, limits and current doctrine on reservation are detailed; never answer a current reservation problem from a slogan alone.

### Article 17: abolition of untouchability
Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. Parliament has enacted legislation to address relevant conduct, including the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955. Current criminal or civil consequences must be checked under the statute applicable to the facts.

### Article 18: abolition of titles
The State may not confer titles except military or academic distinctions, and the Article regulates acceptance of titles and foreign titles in its terms. National awards have been considered in *Balaji Raghavan v Union of India* (1996); distinguish awards from prohibited titles and state the holding carefully.

## 5. Freedoms — Article 19
Article 19(1) guarantees specified freedoms to **citizens**: speech and expression; peaceful assembly without arms; associations/unions/co-operative societies; movement throughout India; residence and settlement; and profession, occupation, trade or business. The original property-related freedom was removed by the 44th Amendment; property is now protected principally through Article 300A, not as an Article 19(1) Fundamental Right.

Each freedom has its own constitutionally listed grounds of restriction. For example, Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions on speech and expression on specified grounds, including sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. Do not import a ground listed for one freedom into another clause unless the text allows it.

A strong Article 19 answer:
1. identify the particular freedom;
2. determine whether it is implicated and whether the claimant is within the right’s personal scope;
3. identify the restriction and statutory basis;
4. identify the exact permitted constitutional ground;
5. apply the relevant reasonableness/proportionality test and precedent;
6. conclude on validity/remedy.

Key cases for selected freedoms include *Romesh Thappar v State of Madras* (1950), *Bennett Coleman & Co. v Union of India* (1972), *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015), and *Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India* (2020). Their holdings are distinct; read the full judgments before attributing detailed tests.

## 6. Protection in criminal matters — Article 20
Article 20 protects against:
- **Article 20(1):** conviction for an act that was not an offence when done and imposition of a greater penalty than the one applicable at the time of the offence (constitutional text governs precise scope).
- **Article 20(2):** prosecution and punishment for the same offence more than once (double jeopardy protection in its constitutional wording).
- **Article 20(3):** compulsion to be a witness against oneself.

Article 20 applies to all persons, not only citizens. The scope of “prosecution”, “punishment” and testimonial compulsion depends on case law. A statement is not automatically protected merely because it is incriminating; constitutional doctrine distinguishes testimonial compulsion from physical evidence and other categories.

Cases often discussed include *Maqbool Hussain v State of Bombay* (1953), *State of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961), and *Selvi v State of Karnataka* (2010). Confirm the precise holding for the question asked.

## 7. Life and personal liberty — Article 21
Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. After *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978), the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable; Articles 14, 19 and 21 may need to be considered together depending on the claim.

Judicial interpretation has recognized a range of interests connected to dignity and liberty, but not every desirable interest automatically becomes an independently enforceable Article 21 right. The claim must be tied to constitutional doctrine and precedent.

Important illustrations:
- *A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras* (1950): early, more compartmentalized approach to rights, later substantially reshaped.
- *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978): fairness in procedure and interconnected rights analysis.
- *Francis Coralie Mullin v Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi* (1981): dignity-related understanding of life.
- *Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985): livelihood and life, with important limits.
- *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India* (2017): privacy as a constitutionally protected right.
- *Common Cause v Union of India* (2018): constitutional discussion of passive euthanasia/advance directives; use the current full judgment and subsequent legal position for detailed questions.

Article 21 doctrine is dynamic. For a current problem, identify the precise claimed right, the State action, applicable precedent and permissible limitations.

## 8. Arrest and detention safeguards — Article 22
Article 22 includes safeguards concerning arrest and detention, including information about grounds of arrest, access to a legal practitioner and production before a magistrate within the constitutionally prescribed period, subject to its text and exceptions. It also contains a separate preventive-detention framework. Do not state that every Article 22 safeguard applies identically to preventive detention; read clauses 1–7 carefully.

Criminal-procedure legislation has changed in 2024: the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure for relevant new matters, subject to commencement and savings. This paper asks constitutional questions, so distinguish the constitutional guarantee in Article 22 from the statutory procedure and verify which procedural code applies to the facts.

## 9. Rights against exploitation — Articles 23–24
- **Article 23:** prohibits trafficking in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour; its scope includes specified protection against private conduct.
- **Article 24:** prohibits employment of children below the constitutional age in factories, mines or other hazardous employment as stated in the text. Related statutory child-labour rules must be checked separately.

Cases such as *People’s Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India* (1982) and *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India* (1984) are useful for forced labour and enforcement, but do not rely on a case name alone; explain the actual issue and principle.

## 10. Freedom of religion — Articles 25–28
- **Article 25:** freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other constitutional provisions. The text also allows specified regulation and social-reform measures.
- **Article 26:** rights of religious denominations to manage religious affairs, subject to constitutional limits.
- **Article 27:** prohibits compelling a person to pay taxes specifically appropriated for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion, according to its wording.
- **Article 28:** regulates religious instruction and worship in certain educational institutions.

Courts distinguish religious belief, practice, denominational autonomy, secular activity and State regulation. The “essential religious practices” doctrine is a contested and evolving line of jurisprudence; do not present it as a simple universal formula. Cases often discussed include *Shirur Mutt* (1954), *Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977), and *Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala* (2018); explain the exact issue and opinions before using them.

## 11. Cultural and educational rights — Articles 29–30
Article 29 protects specified interests relating to language, script and culture and addresses admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on listed grounds. Article 30 protects the rights of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, subject to the constitutional text and judicially recognized regulation.

Do not state that every cultural or educational claim is exclusively a minority right: Article 29(1) is framed for any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture. Article 30 specifically addresses minorities.

## 12. Constitutional remedies — Article 32
Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and empowers it to issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari. Article 226 gives High Courts writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”, so its textual reach is wider, though jurisdiction and discretionary doctrines matter.

Writ memory aid:
- **Habeas corpus:** tests the legality of detention.
- **Mandamus:** commands performance of a public/legal duty, subject to limits.
- **Prohibition:** restrains a body from proceeding beyond jurisdiction before completion.
- **Certiorari:** quashes an order/decision for jurisdictional or other recognized legal error.
- **Quo warranto:** tests the legal authority by which a person holds a public office.
The exact availability of a writ depends on facts, respondent, nature of duty and jurisdiction; avoid absolute one-line definitions.

Article 33 allows Parliament to modify the application of rights to armed forces and specified services to ensure proper discharge of duties and discipline. Article 34 addresses restrictions while martial law is in force in an area in its terms. Article 35 gives Parliament specified legislative powers regarding certain Part III matters.

## 13. Model 20-mark answer
### Q. Explain the scope and significance of Article 21 in light of leading decisions.
**Introduction.** Article 21 protects every person against deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Its interpretation has developed significantly through Supreme Court decisions.

**Early approach.** In *A.K. Gopalan*, the Court adopted a more compartmentalized approach to constitutional rights. Later doctrine moved away from treating each guarantee as entirely isolated.

**Maneka Gandhi.** In *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India*, the Court held that procedure under Article 21 must be fair, just and reasonable and connected the interpretation of Articles 14, 19 and 21. State action affecting liberty must therefore satisfy the relevant constitutional standards, not merely point to a procedure in formal terms.

**Dignity and development.** Later cases connected life with dignity and practical conditions of human existence. *Olga Tellis* discussed livelihood in relation to life while not creating an unrestricted right to occupy public pavements. *Puttaswamy* recognized privacy as a constitutionally protected right grounded in liberty and dignity.

**Limits and method.** Article 21 is not an automatic answer to every social problem. The claimant must identify the State action, the claimed interest, the applicable doctrine and any permissible limitations. Statutory procedure and later judgments must be considered.

**Conclusion.** Article 21 has evolved from a narrower procedural guarantee into a central protection of life, liberty and dignity. Its scope is developed through constitutional text and precedent, with careful attention to the facts and limits of each right.

## 15. Quick recall
- Part III: Articles 12–35.
- Article 12: State; Article 13: inconsistent laws.
- Articles 14–18: equality.
- Article 19: six specified freedoms for citizens, each with its own restriction clause.
- Article 20: retrospective criminal law, double jeopardy, self-incrimination.
- Article 21: life and personal liberty; fair, just and reasonable procedure after *Maneka Gandhi*.
- Article 22: arrest/detention safeguards and preventive detention framework.
- Articles 23–24: exploitation.
- Articles 25–28: religion.
- Articles 29–30: cultural/educational rights.
- Article 32: Supreme Court remedy for Fundamental Rights; Article 226: wider High Court writ text.
- Article 368 amendment power is subject to basic structure; Article 13(4) distinguishes constitutional amendments from ordinary laws for Article 13 purposes.
