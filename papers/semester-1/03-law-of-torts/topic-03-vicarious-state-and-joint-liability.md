## 1. Vicarious liability: concept and rationale
Vicarious liability makes one person legally responsible for a tort committed by another because of a qualifying relationship and a sufficient connection between the wrong and the relevant activity. The underlying tort must first be established. The doctrine can improve access to compensation, encourage employers to manage risk, and allocate losses to enterprises that create and benefit from work-related activities.

## 2. Employer and employee
A claimant usually needs to establish:
1. An employee or other qualifying actor committed a tort.
2. The defendant had the legally relevant relationship with that actor.
3. The tort was committed in the course of employment or was sufficiently connected to the assigned work under the applicable test.
4. The claimant suffered legally recoverable harm.

### Course of employment
An employer may be liable for an unauthorized method of doing assigned work if the act remains sufficiently connected with the job. The employer is not automatically liable for a wholly personal act unrelated to work.

**Illustration:** A delivery employee negligently injures a pedestrian while delivering goods: strong connection to assigned work. If the employee abandons the delivery route for an unrelated personal errand, the connection needs closer analysis.

### Independent contractors
An employer is not ordinarily vicariously liable for every tort of an independent contractor. Exceptions may arise under statute, for non-delegable duties, inherently hazardous activities or the employer's own negligence. Identify the legal basis rather than assuming control alone resolves the question.

## 3. State liability in tort: historical development
The traditional sovereign/non-sovereign distinction arose in older common-law and colonial contexts. Indian courts have narrowed broad immunity and developed public-law compensation for serious constitutional violations. Distinguish:
- ordinary private-law tort damages against the State;
- statutory claims or compensation schemes;
- public-law compensation for clear violations of fundamental rights under Articles 32 or 226;
- personal liability of public officials, which raises separate statutory and procedural issues.

### Leading cases and principles
**State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962):** The State was held liable for negligence by a government driver in circumstances involving a non-sovereign function. It supports accountability for ordinary governmental activity.

**Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of Uttar Pradesh (1965):** The Court applied sovereign-immunity reasoning to property lost in police custody in the circumstances of that case. The decision is historically important and controversial; do not present it as a blanket statement of current immunity.

**N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v State of Andhra Pradesh (1994):** The Supreme Court emphasized limits on immunity and accountability, rejecting the idea that every governmental act is automatically protected.

**Rudul Sah v State of Bihar (1983):** Public-law compensation was awarded for continued detention after acquittal, illustrating constitutional compensation for serious liberty violations.

**Bhim Singh v State of Jammu & Kashmir (1985):** Compensation was awarded for wrongful detention of a legislator, illustrating a constitutional remedy for violation of personal liberty.

### How to analyze a State-liability problem
1. Identify the act/omission and the harm.
2. Identify the relevant duty, tort or statutory obligation.
3. Determine whether the claim is private-law, statutory or constitutional.
4. Examine the nature of the governmental function and any valid immunity.
5. Check statutory notice, limitation, forum and procedural requirements.
6. Consider whether constitutional compensation is appropriate; do not assume every tort is a fundamental-rights violation.

## 4. Joint tortfeasors
Two or more defendants may be responsible for the same tort or indivisible harm. Their liability may be joint and several under the applicable doctrine, allowing the claimant to seek the recoverable amount from one or more responsible parties, subject to rules against double recovery. Contribution between defendants is a separate issue governed by applicable law.

**Distinguish:**
- **Joint tortfeasors:** defendants whose responsibility is legally connected to the same tort.
- **Concurrent causes:** separate acts may contribute to one injury; whether defendants are jointly liable depends on causation and doctrine.
- **Mere association:** friendship, presence or relationship with a tortfeasor is not enough by itself.

## 5. Non-delegable duties and independent contractors
Some duties cannot be discharged simply by hiring another person to perform the work. Whether a non-delegable duty exists depends on statute and legal doctrine, including the nature of the activity, vulnerability of those affected and the defendant's undertaking. Hazardous activities may attract special duties and statutory responsibility.

## 6. Exam comparison: direct and vicarious liability
Direct liability asks whether the defendant personally breached a duty or committed a tort. Vicarious liability asks whether the law attributes another person's tort to the defendant because of their relationship and the connection to work. An employer can face both where it negligently hired, trained or supervised a worker and is also vicariously responsible for the worker's tort.

## 7. Worked problem
**Problem:** A municipal employee driving a department vehicle negligently injures a pedestrian while carrying out assigned official work. The municipality argues that only the employee is liable.

**Answer:** The pedestrian must prove the driver's negligence, causation and damage. Because the driving occurred while carrying out assigned work, the connection to employment is strong. The State-liability analysis then asks whether any valid statutory immunity applies and whether the act was a governmental function protected by a specific rule; broad immunity should not be assumed. *Vidyawati* and *N. Nagendra Rao* are relevant starting points, while *Kasturi Lal* must be explained in its historical context. Depending on the legal framework and facts, the municipality/State may be liable as well as the driver.
