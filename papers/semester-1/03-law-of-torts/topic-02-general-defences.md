## 1. Purpose of general defences
A defence may show that the defendant's conduct was justified, that the claimant accepted a risk, that the harm could not reasonably have been prevented, that legislation authorized the conduct, or that the claimant's own fault should reduce recovery. Defences are not interchangeable. State the legal test and apply each element to the facts.

## 2. *Volenti non fit injuria* — voluntary assumption of risk
The maxim means that no legal injury is done to a person who freely and knowingly consents to the relevant risk.

### Elements
1. The claimant knew the nature and extent of the relevant risk.
2. The claimant freely agreed to accept that risk; knowledge alone is insufficient.
3. The injury arose within the scope of the risk accepted.
4. No statute or public policy rule bars the defence.

### Limits and examples
- A spectator may accept ordinary risks inherent in a sport, not every reckless or negligent act.
- An employee's continued work despite knowledge of danger does not automatically prove free consent, especially where economic pressure or lack of meaningful choice exists.
- A warning is relevant evidence but does not itself establish a valid agreement.
- Consent obtained by coercion, fraud or material misrepresentation may be invalid.

**Exam distinction:** *Volenti* can defeat liability if fully established. Contributory negligence generally concerns failure to take reasonable care for one's own safety and usually reduces damages under the applicable law; it is not identical to consent.

## 3. Necessity
Necessity may justify interference with a right where action is reasonably required to prevent a greater and imminent harm. The response must be proportionate and connected to the emergency. It is narrow, not a general licence to interfere with another's property for convenience.

**Illustration:** Breaking a locked door to rescue a person from a fire may be defensible if the entry was reasonably necessary. Breaking into a property merely to save time is not necessity.

**Exam points:** Was the danger imminent? Was the act reasonably necessary? Was the response proportionate? Was there a less harmful practical alternative? Is the defence recognized for the particular tort?

## 4. Inevitable accident
An inevitable accident is an event that could not have been avoided despite reasonable care. The defendant must show more than that the event was unintended or unusual.

**Application:** If a driver maintained the vehicle properly and a latent, undetectable failure caused an accident, the defence may be argued, depending on evidence and the applicable duty. If a reasonable inspection would have discovered the defect, the defence is unlikely to succeed.

**Distinguish from Act of God:** Inevitable accident can arise from human or natural causes; Act of God concerns an extraordinary natural event.

## 5. Act of God
An Act of God is an extraordinary natural event that could not reasonably have been foreseen or prevented and that causes the damage. Ordinary rain, predictable seasonal conditions or foreseeable flooding are not automatically Acts of God. If the defendant's negligence contributed to the harm, the defence may fail.

**Elements to discuss:** natural cause; exceptional/unforeseeable character under the relevant legal test; inability to prevent despite reasonable care; causal connection; absence of a material negligent contribution.

## 6. Statutory authority
Where an Act authorizes an activity expressly or by necessary implication, liability may be excluded for consequences that are unavoidable when the statutory power is properly exercised. The defence is not automatic merely because a public body or licensed operator was involved.

Check:
- Was the activity authorized by the statute?
- Were statutory conditions and procedures followed?
- Was the damage an unavoidable consequence of lawful exercise?
- Was there negligent execution or conduct outside the power?
- Does the statute preserve compensation or provide a special remedy?

## 7. Plaintiff's own fault: contributory negligence
Contributory negligence arises where the claimant fails to take reasonable care for their own safety and that failure contributes to the injury. Under the applicable rules, it may reduce damages. It should be distinguished from the defendant's negligence and from voluntary acceptance of risk.

**Illustration:** A pedestrian carelessly crosses a road while a speeding driver also fails to keep a proper lookout. Depending on the evidence, the pedestrian's conduct may affect apportionment without excusing the driver's breach.

## 8. Illegality and public policy
Some claims may be affected by illegality or public policy, but there is no safe universal statement that any illegal conduct bars recovery. The court considers the particular cause of action, statutory scheme and policy of the rule. Do not invoke illegality without identifying the relevant doctrine.

## 9. Distinguishing the principal defences

| Defence | Core question | Likely legal effect |
|---|---|---|
| *Volenti* | Did claimant freely and knowingly accept this risk? | May defeat the claim if fully established |
| Necessity | Was interference reasonably needed to avert greater imminent harm? | May justify otherwise wrongful conduct |
| Inevitable accident | Could reasonable care have prevented the event? | May negate breach/liability |
| Act of God | Did an extraordinary natural event cause harm that reasonable care could not prevent? | May excuse liability under the applicable rule |
| Statutory authority | Was the harmful activity lawfully authorized and the harm unavoidable? | May exclude liability within statutory limits |
| Contributory negligence | Did claimant's lack of reasonable self-care contribute to the harm? | Usually reduces damages under applicable law |

## 10. Case-law cautions
Defences are highly fact-sensitive. Use cases prescribed by the course and verify the actual ratio. Do not cite a case name merely because it is associated with a maxim; explain the factual analogy and why it fits. For *volenti*, distinguish consent to ordinary risk from consent to negligence; for statutory authority, distinguish unavoidable consequences from negligent execution.

## 11. Practice questions

### Q1. Explain *volenti non fit injuria* and distinguish it from contributory negligence.
**Answer plan:** Define voluntary assumption of risk; set out knowledge, freedom and scope; explain knowledge is not consent; compare complete defence with reduction of damages; apply a sports/workplace/transport illustration.

### Q2. Distinguish Act of God and inevitable accident.
**Answer plan:** Define both; identify natural-event requirement for Act of God; explain inevitable accident can have human or natural cause; show that reasonable care and foreseeability are central.

### Q3. When does statutory authority protect a defendant?
**Answer plan:** Show express/implied authority; proper exercise; unavoidable consequence; no negligent execution/out-of-power conduct; check whether statute preserves compensation.

### Q4. A passenger knows a bus has defective brakes but boards because there is no practical alternative. The bus crashes.
**Answer:** The operator may have breached its duty by using a dangerously defective bus. The passenger's knowledge is relevant but does not alone establish voluntary consent; lack of meaningful choice weighs against *volenti*. Contributory negligence requires a separate assessment of whether the passenger acted unreasonably in the circumstances. The operator's duty and breach remain central.
