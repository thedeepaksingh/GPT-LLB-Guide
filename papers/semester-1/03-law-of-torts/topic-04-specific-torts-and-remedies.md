## 1. Negligence

Negligence is failure to take the care that the law requires in the circumstances, causing legally recognized damage. The usual elements are duty, breach, factual/legal causation and damage.

### Duty of care
Ask whether the law recognizes a duty in the relationship and circumstances. Foreseeability matters but may not be the only consideration; proximity, established categories, policy and statute can matter.

### Breach
Compare the defendant's conduct with the reasonable standard in context. Consider likelihood and seriousness of harm, practicality and cost of precautions, and purpose of the activity. Professional negligence requires the applicable professional standard; a poor outcome alone is not proof of breach.

### Causation and damage
Factual causation often asks whether the injury would have occurred but for the breach. Legal causation and remoteness then limit liability. Negligence generally requires actual legally recognized damage.

### *Res ipsa loquitur*
“The thing speaks for itself” is an evidential inference where the accident ordinarily would not occur without negligence, the relevant instrumentality was under the defendant's management/control, and evidence does not reveal a better explanation. *Byrne v Boadle* (falling barrel) is a classic illustration. It is not a separate tort or automatic liability.

### Medical negligence
*Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab* (2005) is an important Supreme Court authority discussing professional negligence and cautioning against equating every error or unsuccessful treatment with negligence. Civil, consumer, disciplinary and criminal standards must be kept distinct.

## 2. Nuisance

Nuisance concerns interference with use or enjoyment of land or a public right.

### Private nuisance
Typically requires substantial and unreasonable interference with the claimant's use/enjoyment of land and a sufficient interest in the land. Examples include persistent noise, smoke, vibration, pollution or water seepage. Relevant considerations may include locality, duration, intensity, frequency, social utility and sensitivity.

### Public nuisance
Affects the public or a class of the public, such as obstruction of a public highway or conditions creating a community health hazard. A private claimant ordinarily needs special damage beyond that suffered by the public generally unless a statute supplies another route.

### Remedies and case
Damages and injunctions may be available; statutory abatement routes may also apply. *Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand* (1980) illustrates judicial enforcement of municipal statutory duties in a public-nuisance/sanitation setting. Mere public utility does not automatically legalize substantial unlawful interference.

## 3. Trespass to land and goods

### Trespass to land
Direct, unjustified interference with possession, including unauthorized entry, remaining after permission ends or placing an object on land. It is generally actionable per se. Possession is often central; absolute ownership is not always required to sue.

### Trespass to goods and conversion
Trespass to goods involves direct interference with movable property. Conversion is a more serious dealing inconsistent with the claimant's rights. Historical terminology such as detinue should be used carefully and with the applicable modern procedural/statutory framework in view.

### Defences
Consent, lawful authority and narrow necessity arguments may apply. An honest mistake is not automatically a defence to direct unauthorized entry.

## 4. Trespass to person: assault, battery and false imprisonment

### Assault
An act that causes reasonable apprehension of imminent unlawful force. The claimant need not be physically struck. Context matters; mere insulting words may not suffice without an imminent threat or act.

### Battery
Intentional or otherwise legally actionable direct application of unlawful force without consent or justification. Physical injury is not always essential. Consent and proportionate self-defence may be relevant.

### False imprisonment
Total restraint of freedom of movement without lawful justification. It may be imposed by force, barriers or asserted authority depending on circumstances. In *Bird v Jones*, partial obstruction with an alternative route was distinguished from total restraint.

### Malicious prosecution
Generally requires: institution or active instigation of proceedings by defendant; termination in claimant's favour; absence of reasonable and probable cause; malice; and legally recognized damage. Acquittal alone is not enough.

## 5. Defamation

Defamation protects reputation from legally wrongful publication. A claimant generally must establish a defamatory imputation concerning them and publication to a third party. The precise legal requirements and defences depend on the cause of action and applicable Indian law.

### Defamatory meaning and identification
Assess the words in context and the meaning conveyed to an ordinary recipient. A claimant may be identifiable without being named if readers reasonably understand the reference.

### Publication
Communication to a third party is ordinarily central. Digital content may raise authorship, reach, repetition and intermediary questions; apply the relevant legal framework to the facts.

### Defences
Potential defences include truth/justification, honest opinion/fair comment, privilege and other recognized grounds. Each has conditions. Public interest is not a magic phrase that automatically defeats liability. Distinguish civil defamation from criminal defamation and verify the criminal provision applicable to the relevant date; do not automatically use old IPC numbering where BNS applies.

## 6. Remoteness and thin-skull rule

Remoteness limits recovery for consequences too remote under the applicable tort rule. *The Wagon Mound (No. 1)* is commonly cited for foreseeability of the type of damage in negligence. The thin-skull principle means that, once a foreseeable kind of personal injury is established, a defendant may be responsible for the full extent even where the claimant has unusual vulnerability; *Smith v Leech Brain & Co.* is a familiar illustration.

Separate factual causation, type of harm, extent of harm and any intervening act.

## 7. Remedies

- Compensatory damages for proven legally recoverable loss.
- Nominal damages for certain rights infringements without substantial loss.
- Aggravated/exemplary damages only where legally available and justified.
- Injunctions for continuing or threatened interference.
- Statutory remedies where a specialized Act applies.
- Public-law compensation for certain clear fundamental-rights violations, distinct from ordinary tort damages.

Remedies depend on proof, causation, mitigation, limitation, the nature of the right and any statutory scheme.

## 8. Worked problems

### Problem A: A barrel falls from a warehouse and injures a passer-by.
**Answer:** The claimant must identify duty, breach, causation and damage. If the event ordinarily would not occur without negligence and the barrel was under the warehouse operator's control, *res ipsa loquitur* may permit an inference of negligence, unless the defendant provides a satisfactory explanation. The inference is evidential, not automatic.

### Problem B: A factory emits persistent noise at night, seriously interfering with a neighbour's use of their home.
**Answer:** Consider private nuisance: substantial and unreasonable interference with use/enjoyment of land, the claimant's sufficient interest, duration/intensity/locality, causation and possible statutory authority. Remedies may include damages and an injunction shaped to the circumstances.

### Problem C: A person blocks one exit but the claimant can freely leave through another.
**Answer:** False imprisonment ordinarily requires total restraint. An available reasonable alternative route may mean the restraint is partial rather than total, following the principle illustrated by *Bird v Jones*. Consider threats, physical circumstances and whether the alternative was genuinely accessible.

### Problem D: A newspaper falsely accuses a named person of fraud.
**Answer:** Analyze identification, defamatory meaning, publication to third parties, evidence of loss and defences such as truth, privilege or honest opinion. An allegation of fraud is capable of damaging reputation, but liability depends on the actual wording, context, proof and applicable cause of action.

## 9. Practice questions

### Q1. Explain the elements of negligence and *res ipsa loquitur*.
**Answer plan:** Duty; breach; causation; damage; remoteness; explain evidential inference and limits; apply a falling-object or accident example.

### Q2. Distinguish private and public nuisance.
**Answer plan:** Protected interest; claimant standing; nature of interference; special damage for private action in public nuisance; remedies; *Ratlam* example.

### Q3. Explain assault, battery and false imprisonment.
**Answer plan:** Define each; identify apprehension/imminent force, unlawful contact and total restraint; defences; give a separate example.

### Q4. State the elements and defences of defamation.
**Answer plan:** Identification, defamatory meaning, publication; explain truth, opinion, privilege; distinguish civil and criminal routes; apply facts.

### Q5. Explain remoteness and the thin-skull rule.
**Answer plan:** Factual causation vs legal remoteness; *Wagon Mound*; thin-skull principle and *Smith v Leech Brain*; apply to extent/type of harm.
