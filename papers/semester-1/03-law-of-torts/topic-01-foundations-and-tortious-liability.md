## 1. Meaning and nature of tort

A tort is a civil wrong, independent of a mere breach of contract, for which the law provides a civil remedy, commonly unliquidated damages. Tort law protects interests such as bodily integrity, liberty, possession, property, reputation and reasonable use of land. It also allocates risks created by careless conduct and, in defined situations, by dangerous activities or relationships.

India does not have one exhaustive statute codifying all torts. Indian tort law draws heavily on common-law principles, but courts apply them in the Indian constitutional and statutory setting. Specific fields—consumer protection, motor-vehicle compensation, environmental harm and public-law compensation—may be governed or supplemented by legislation.

### Essential vocabulary
- **Civil wrong:** A wrong for which civil proceedings may provide a remedy.
- **Legal right / legal injury:** An interest protected by law; not every moral or factual grievance is a legal injury.
- **Damage:** Loss or harm suffered.
- **Damages:** Monetary compensation awarded by a court.
- **Unliquidated damages:** Amount not fixed in advance and assessed by the court.
- **Actionable per se:** A tort for which infringement itself can support a claim without proof of actual consequential loss.
- **Causation:** The legally relevant connection between the defendant's conduct and the claimant's loss.
- **Defence:** A legal reason why liability is excluded, justified or reduced.

## 2. Core elements of tortious liability

The elements differ from tort to tort, so avoid claiming every element is required in every case. A useful general checklist is:

1. **Protected interest and duty:** Identify the right or interest protected and the legal duty owed.
2. **Wrongful conduct:** An act or omission that meets the elements of the tort. Conduct may be intentional, negligent or subject to a strict-liability rule.
3. **Damage where required:** Negligence normally requires legally recognized damage; trespass to land and false imprisonment may be actionable per se.
4. **Causation:** Establish factual causation and the legally accepted connection between breach and loss.
5. **Remoteness:** Determine whether the kind of harm is too remote under the applicable rule.
6. **Defences and remedies:** Consider consent, necessity, contributory negligence, statutory authority and other applicable defences; then identify the remedy.

**Exam method:** Name the tort first. State its specific elements, apply each element to the facts, address defences and conclude on liability/remedy.

## 3. Tort compared with contract and crime

| Basis | Tort | Contract | Crime |
|---|---|---| 
| Main concern | Civil wrong against a protected interest | Breach of an obligation assumed by agreement | Public wrong defined by penal law |
| Source of duty | Generally imposed by law | Agreement and contract law | Criminal statute/common-law offence as applicable |
| Typical proceeding | Claim by injured party | Claim by contracting party/person with standing | Prosecution ordinarily by State |
| Main outcome | Damages, injunction and other civil relief | Contractual damages, specific performance where available, etc. | Punishment; compensation may also be ordered where law permits |
| Standard of proof | Balance of probabilities in civil proceedings | Balance of probabilities in civil proceedings | Beyond reasonable doubt for guilt |
| Overlap | Same facts may also breach contract or constitute crime | Same facts may also be tortious | Same conduct may also support civil claims |

A negligent professional may breach a contract and commit a tort if the elements of both are established. An assault may be a crime and a tort. The causes of action must be analyzed separately; proof of one does not automatically prove the other.

## 4. Legal damage: the two maxims

### A. *Damnum sine injuria* — loss without legal injury
This means factual loss without infringement of a legal right. A business may lose customers because a competitor lawfully opens nearby. Economic loss alone does not turn lawful conduct into a tort.

**Illustration:** In the *Gloucester Grammar School Case* (1410), a rival school charged lower fees and the existing school suffered loss. The competition was not actionable merely because it harmed the incumbent.

**Principle:** Tort law does not compensate every disadvantage; it responds to legally recognized wrongs.

### B. *Injuria sine damno* — legal injury without measurable loss
This means infringement of a legal right even though the claimant cannot prove measurable financial loss. Torts actionable per se protect the right itself.

**Illustration:** In *Ashby v White* (1703), a qualified voter was wrongfully prevented from voting. The violation of the right was actionable even though the claimant could not show that the election result would have changed.

**Important qualification:** The maxim does not erase a damage requirement where the particular tort requires damage. It applies where the law treats the infringement itself as actionable.

## 5. Fault, intention, negligence, motive and malice

### Fault
Fault broadly concerns blameworthy conduct, often through intention or failure to take reasonable care. Some liability rules do not require proof of ordinary negligence, so do not treat fault as a universal element.

### Intention
Intention relates to the result or interference intended by the defendant. The exact mental element varies by tort. Intentional interference with a protected right may be actionable even if the defendant did not intend the full extent of loss.

### Negligence
Negligence generally requires duty, breach of the reasonable-care standard, causation and damage. An accident or bad outcome does not, by itself, prove negligence.

### Motive
Motive is the reason for acting. A bad motive does not ordinarily make lawful conduct tortious, and a good motive does not excuse an established tort. Motive can matter where malice, improper purpose or good faith is legally relevant.

### Malice
“Malice” is context-sensitive. Actual ill-will may be called malice in fact; malice in law is a technical expression whose meaning depends on the doctrine. For malicious prosecution, malice and absence of reasonable and probable cause are separate issues. Do not use “malice” as a substitute for proving each element.

## 6. Actionable per se and damage-dependent torts

**Often actionable per se:** trespass to land, false imprisonment, and some direct interferences with person or legal rights. Nominal damages may be available where a right was infringed but substantial loss is not proved.

**Usually damage-dependent:** negligence ordinarily requires actual legally recognized harm. A careless act that causes no damage may not support a negligence damages claim, although another tort or statutory remedy might apply.

This distinction explains why the two maxims are not contradictory: one concerns lawful conduct causing loss; the other concerns an actionable infringement for which actual loss need not be proved.

## 7. Who may sue and who may be sued?

Standing and capacity are tort-specific. A person whose legally protected interest is infringed may generally sue, subject to limitation, legal-personality and procedural rules. Minors may sue through an appropriate representative. Companies and other legal persons can sue for recognized interests such as property or reputation. Claims after death, claims by dependants, and claims against public authorities may be governed by specific statutes or procedural requirements.

Potential defendants include individuals, employers, corporations and public authorities, subject to the elements of the relevant tort and any valid statutory protections. Do not assume all claims survive death or that the State is always immune.

## 8. Joint tortfeasors

Two or more persons may be responsible for the same tort or indivisible injury. Depending on the doctrine and statute, liability to the claimant may be joint and several, while contribution between defendants is a separate question. Mere presence at the scene or association with the wrongdoer does not automatically make a person liable: each defendant must have a legally sufficient basis for responsibility.

## 9. Causation and remoteness — overview

- **Factual causation:** Did the conduct cause the harm under the applicable test, commonly the “but for” test?
- **Legal causation:** Is the connection sufficiently direct or legally attributable under the relevant doctrine?
- **Remoteness:** Is the kind of damage within the legally recoverable scope?
- **Intervening act:** Did a later event break the chain of responsibility?
- **Thin-skull principle:** Once injury of a foreseeable kind is established, the defendant may have to take the claimant as found, including unusual vulnerability.

In *The Wagon Mound (No. 1)*, the Privy Council is commonly cited for foreseeability of the kind of damage in negligence. The exact test can differ by tort and authority.

## 10. Remedies in tort

- **Compensatory damages:** Aim to compensate legally recoverable loss.
- **Nominal damages:** Recognize infringement of a right where substantial loss is not proved.
- **Aggravated or exemplary damages:** Available only where the legal requirements for the relevant category are met.
- **Injunction:** Orders a person to stop or refrain from conduct, often relevant to nuisance or continuing interference.
- **Restitution / return of property:** May be available where the cause of action and procedure permit.
- **Statutory compensation:** May be available under consumer, motor-vehicle, environmental or other legislation.
- **Public-law compensation:** Constitutional courts may award compensation for certain serious violations of fundamental rights; this is distinct from ordinary private-law tort damages.

Mitigation, proof, causation, limitation and the nature of the right affect relief.

## 12. Practice questions with answer plans

### Q1. Define tort and discuss its essential elements.
**Answer plan:** Define tort; note absence of exhaustive codification in India; explain protected interest/duty, wrongful conduct, damage where required, causation, remoteness and defences; distinguish actionable-per-se torts; conclude that elements depend on the specific tort.

### Q2. Distinguish *damnum sine injuria* and *injuria sine damno*.
**Answer plan:** Define both maxims; explain *Gloucester Grammar School Case* and *Ashby v White*; contrast lawful loss with infringement of a protected right; explain actionable per se; conclude with the need to prove the elements of the particular tort.

### Q3. Distinguish tort, crime and contract.
**Answer plan:** Compare purpose, source of duty, parties, standard of proof and remedies; mention overlapping liability; apply with a short example.

### Q4. Does bad motive establish tortious liability?
**Answer plan:** Explain motive versus intention; general rule that motive does not usually convert lawful conduct into a tort; identify exceptions where malice/improper purpose is an element; distinguish malicious prosecution.

### Q5. Explain who may sue and be sued in tort.
**Answer plan:** Explain protected interest and standing; legal persons/minors/representatives; corporate and public-authority liability; flag statutory rules for death, limitation and capacity.
